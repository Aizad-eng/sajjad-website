How to choose a blood pressure monitor and pulse oximeter

Updated November 1, 2026 · 9 min read

Quick answer

Choose a blood pressure monitor that appears on an independent validated list such as STRIDE BP, validatebp.org or the British and Irish Hypertension Society list, preferably an upper-arm model with a cuff that fits the user's arm. For a pulse oximeter, look for a device made for medical purposes, with stated accuracy (Arms) and testing to ISO 80601-2-61.

Blood pressure monitors and pulse oximeters look simple, and many cheap models are sold online. The hard part is accuracy. This guide explains how to check whether a blood pressure monitor has been properly validated, how to pick the cuff size, how pulse oximeter accuracy is stated and what changed in the US FDA's 2025 proposals on skin pigmentation. It is a buying guide and does not replace advice from a doctor about readings.

What makes a blood pressure monitor "validated"?

A validated monitor has been tested against reference measurements in a clinical study that follows a recognised protocol. The current international protocol is the AAMI/ESH/ISO Universal Standard, published as ISO 81060-2:2018. It was agreed by the US Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation, the European Society of Hypertension and ISO to replace earlier separate protocols.

Validation matters because many devices on the market have never been through such a study. A 2021 review by STRIDE BP, a scientific non-profit that works with the European Society of Hypertension, the International Society of Hypertension and the World Hypertension League, states that very few devices available on the market have been validated using an established protocol. The same review notes that many published validation studies were rejected for poor study quality, not only for device inaccuracy.

A claim such as "clinically tested" on the box is not the same as an independent validation listing. Ask for the model's listing instead.

The British Heart Foundation advises choosing a monitor that has been independently tested and validated, and points to the BIHS and STRIDE lists. Search the exact model number, because a brand may have both validated and unvalidated models with similar names.

Should you choose an upper-arm or a wrist monitor?

The British Heart Foundation advises that, ideally, you should choose a monitor that measures at the upper arm, with a wrist cuff as an alternative when an upper-arm cuff is not possible. STRIDE BP only gives its "Preferred" label to devices with an upper-arm cuff. Its preferred criteria also include at least one successful validation published in the last 10 years and, for home monitors, automatic storage of multiple readings or connectivity.

Most validation work has been done on upper-arm devices. In the STRIDE BP review of 419 validation studies, 81 percent tested upper-arm cuff devices, 17 percent wrist devices and 2 percent finger devices.

How do you choose the right cuff size?

Cuff size matters as much as the monitor. A study reported in JAMA Internal Medicine measured the upper-arm circumference of 195 adults and took readings with different cuffs. Using a regular cuff on people who needed a large one overestimated systolic pressure by 4.8 points. On people who needed an extra-large cuff, the overestimate was 19.5 points. A cuff that was too large for small arms underestimated systolic pressure by 3.6 points.

Cuff ranges differ by brand, so always match the measured arm to the range printed on the cuff. The British Heart Foundation notes that most monitors come with a medium cuff, so people with much larger or smaller arms may need to buy a different cuff separately, and that the cuff should fit snugly with room to slide two fingers underneath. For a clinic, it is worth keeping at least small, regular and large cuffs that fit your monitor.

How accurate are pulse oximeters?

A pulse oximeter uses light to estimate blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) and pulse rate, usually through a fingertip. The FDA stresses that readings are estimates. It lists poor circulation, skin pigmentation, skin thickness, skin temperature, current tobacco use and fingernail polish as factors that can affect accuracy.

Accuracy is stated as Arms, the root mean square difference between the device reading and arterial blood oxygen measured in a lab study. The international standard for pulse oximeter safety and performance is ISO 80601-2-61, and its Annex EE describes the controlled desaturation study used to measure accuracy. A lower Arms means a more accurate device. The FDA's 2025 draft guidance recommends an overall Arms below 3 percent for the sensor types it lists.

What did the FDA propose in 2025 about skin pigmentation?

In January 2025 the FDA issued draft guidance titled "Pulse Oximeters for Medical Purposes". Its main points:

  • Accuracy studies should assess skin tone with both the Monk Skin Tone scale and an objective measure called the individual typology angle.
  • Studies should include a diversely pigmented group of 150 or more healthy participants.
  • Devices that show comparable performance across skin tones should state this prominently on the label.
  • When final, it will replace the FDA's 2013 guidance on pulse oximeter submissions.

When this guide was written, the FDA guidance page still listed the document as a draft. The FDA's 2021 safety communication had already warned that pulse oximeters have limitations and can be inaccurate in some circumstances. Check the current status on the FDA website before relying on it in a tender.

Should you buy a fingertip or a handheld pulse oximeter?

Fingertip oximeters combine the sensor and display in one clip that sits on the finger. Other pulse oximeter systems use separate sensors, which the FDA's draft guidance groups as transmittance sensors (wrap and clip), ear clips and reflectance sensors. The draft also recommends physiological alarms for pulse oximeters used for continuous real-time monitoring.

The bigger question is the intended use. The FDA separates pulse oximeters for medical purposes, mainly used in hospitals and clinics, from products sold for general wellness, sports or aviation. It states that those wellness products are not evaluated for clinical decision-making. Before buying, check the label and manual for a medical-purpose statement and a stated accuracy figure.

What should you specify when ordering?

Blood pressure monitor:

  1. Exact brand and model, with its validation listing (STRIDE BP, validatebp.org or BIHS).
  2. Upper-arm or wrist.
  3. Cuff sizes needed, with arm circumference ranges.
  4. Memory, number of users and data connection if needed.
  5. Power: batteries, adapter or both.

Pulse oximeter:

  1. Fingertip, handheld or bedside.
  2. Stated SpO2 accuracy (Arms) and range, and the standard it is tested to.
  3. Adult or paediatric sensors, and whether sensors are reusable or single use.
  4. Alarms, display type and data storage for clinic use.
  5. Medical-purpose labelling, not a wellness or sports product.

Frequently asked questions

Are wrist blood pressure monitors accurate?

Some wrist monitors are validated, but most validation studies cover upper-arm devices and STRIDE BP only marks upper-arm devices as Preferred. The British Heart Foundation advises an upper-arm monitor where possible.

How do I know if a BP monitor is validated?

Search the exact model on an independent list such as STRIDE BP, validatebp.org or the British and Irish Hypertension Society list. A claim of being clinically tested on the box is not the same.

What cuff size do I need?

Measure around the middle of the upper arm and choose a cuff whose printed range covers that number. A cuff that is too small can overestimate blood pressure.

What does Arms mean on a pulse oximeter?

Arms is the root mean square difference between the oximeter's SpO2 readings and arterial blood oxygen in a lab study. A lower figure means better accuracy.

Do pulse oximeters work on darker skin?

The FDA lists skin pigmentation among factors that can affect accuracy. Its January 2025 draft guidance asks makers to test across a wider range of skin tones and label comparable performance.
