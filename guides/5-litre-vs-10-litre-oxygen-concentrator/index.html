5 litre vs 10 litre oxygen concentrator: the difference and why the prescription decides

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

A 5 litre oxygen concentrator delivers up to 5 litres of oxygen per minute and a 10 litre model up to 10, so the right size depends on the flow rate the doctor prescribes. Ten litre machines usually use about twice the power, weigh more and may not run below 2 litres per minute.

Oxygen concentrators are sold mainly in two sizes, 5 litre and 10 litre. The number is the highest flow the machine can deliver in litres per minute (L/min). This guide compares the two sizes using published manufacturer specifications, explains why the doctor's written prescription decides the size, and lists what to check before you order. It is a buying guide only. It does not tell you how much oxygen anyone should use.

What is the difference between a 5 litre and a 10 litre oxygen concentrator?

The main difference is the maximum flow: a 5 litre concentrator delivers up to 5 L/min and a 10 litre concentrator up to 10 L/min. Both take in room air and deliver oxygen-enriched air through a flowmeter on the front of the machine.

The bigger machine needs a bigger compressor, so it usually uses more electricity and weighs more. You can see this in one manufacturer's own range. Drive DeVilbiss lists its 5 litre model (525DS) at 310 W average and 36 lb (about 16.3 kg), and its 10 litre model (1025DS) at 639 W average and 42 lb (about 19 kg). Drive says the two models have an identical footprint, so the 10 litre unit takes about the same floor space.

How does the prescription decide which size you need?

The doctor or specialist decides the flow rate and hours of use, and the machine you buy must be able to deliver that flow. The UK National Health Service (NHS) says home oxygen should only be used if a GP or specialist has prescribed it. MedlinePlus, from the US National Library of Medicine, tells users not to stop or change their oxygen flow without talking to their provider.

In practice, buyers bring the prescription and check three things against it:

  • The prescribed flow in L/min: it must fall inside the machine's flow range.
  • The hours per day: a machine that runs most of the day and night needs a reliable power plan.
  • Any change the doctor expects: ask the doctor whether the flow is likely to change, and buy for the range they advise.

Do not choose a size on your own because a larger number sounds safer. Follow your doctor's advice on flow and hours.

Can a 10 litre concentrator be used at low flow rates?

Not always, because some 10 litre models have a higher minimum flow than 5 litre models. Drive lists its 1025DS 10 litre machine at 2.0 to 10.0 L/min. Its 525DS 5 litre machine and the Philips EverFlo both start at 0.5 L/min.

So if the prescribed flow is below 2 L/min, a 10 litre model like the 1025DS cannot be set that low. Always read the minimum as well as the maximum on the specification sheet of the exact model you are offered.

How much oxygen purity should a concentrator deliver?

A well-specified concentrator states its oxygen purity at its rated flow, and the models in this guide list figures between 87 and 96 percent. Philips lists the EverFlo at 93 percent plus or minus 3 percent at 5 L/min. Drive lists both the 525DS and the 1025DS at 87 to 96 percent across their flow ranges.

Purity matters more than the litre number on the box. Ask for the purity at the highest flow, not just at low flow. Philips notes that using the machine outside its specified voltage, flow, temperature, humidity or altitude may lower the oxygen concentration. Many models also have an oxygen sensor with alarms. On the EverFlo with its oxygen percentage indicator, the alarms trigger at 82 percent (low) and 70 percent (very low).

What do load-shedding and voltage mean for a concentrator in Pakistan?

A concentrator only works while it has electricity, so you need a backup plan for power cuts. MedlinePlus advises keeping a backup oxygen cylinder in case the power goes out.

If you plan to run the machine on a UPS, inverter or generator, compare its running wattage with the backup's capacity. A 639 W machine needs about twice the backup of a 310 W machine for the same hours. Also check the voltage and frequency on the rating label. The US version of the Drive 1025DS, for example, is rated 120 V, 60 Hz, while the UNFPA specification asks for 220 to 240 V, 50 to 60 Hz, or 110 V. Make sure the version you buy matches your mains supply.

Can an oxygen concentrator run a ventilator or CPAP machine?

No, a standard concentrator is not meant to be the oxygen source for other breathing support equipment. The United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) procurement page states that concentrators are not suitable as an oxygen source for ventilators, CPAP devices or high-flow nasal oxygen. If a hospital needs oxygen for that kind of equipment, it needs a different supply.

What safety rules apply to oxygen concentrators at home?

The main rules are no smoking and no open flames near the machine. The NHS advises keeping the device at least 3 metres from open flames such as gas cookers, and at least 1.5 metres from other electrical appliances and heat sources. It also warns against oil-based products such as petroleum jelly, which are a fire risk.

MedlinePlus adds that nasal tubing should be cleaned weekly, and cannulas or masks replaced every 2 to 4 weeks. Follow the manufacturer's manual for placement and filter cleaning.

What changes the price of a 5 litre or 10 litre concentrator in Pakistan?

The price depends on capacity, brand, features and the exchange rate on the day of import. A 10 litre machine has a larger compressor, so it usually costs more than a 5 litre machine from the same maker. Other things that change the quote:

  • Oxygen sensor and alarms: models with a purity sensor cost more than basic units.
  • Voltage version: the 220 to 240 V version for your supply.
  • Accessories: humidifier bottle, cannulas and spare filters.
  • Quantity and freight: hospitals ordering several units may get a different unit price.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 10 litre oxygen concentrator better than a 5 litre one?

Not by itself. A 10 litre machine delivers a higher maximum flow but uses more power and may have a higher minimum flow, so the right size is the one that covers the flow on the prescription.

Can I run a 5 litre concentrator at 1 litre per minute?

Many 5 litre models start at 0.5 L/min, including the Philips EverFlo and Drive DeVilbiss 525DS. Check the flow range of the exact model and follow your doctor's advice on the setting.

How much electricity does a 10 litre oxygen concentrator use?

Drive lists its 1025DS 10 litre model at 639 W average, compared with 310 W for its 525DS 5 litre model. Check the wattage of the exact model when sizing a UPS or generator.

Do I need a prescription for an oxygen concentrator?

Health services such as the NHS say home oxygen should only be used when a doctor or specialist has prescribed it. The prescription also sets the flow rate the machine must deliver.

Can one oxygen concentrator be used with a ventilator?

No. The UNFPA procurement specification states that oxygen concentrators are not suitable as an oxygen source for ventilators, CPAP devices or high-flow nasal oxygen.
