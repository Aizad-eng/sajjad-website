How to choose a wheelchair: sizes, types and fitting

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

To choose a wheelchair, match it to the user's body, how they move and where they live. The World Health Organization (WHO) uses four measurements: hip width for seat width, pelvis-to-knee length less 30 to 60 mm for seat depth, calf length for footrest height, and back height for the backrest. Then choose frame, wheels and cushion.

A wheelchair that fits well is more comfortable, supports upright sitting and is easier to push. One that does not fit can cause poor posture and pressure sores. This guide follows the World Health Organization (WHO) Wheelchair Service Training Package (Basic Level), which teaches how to measure a user, choose a wheelchair and check the fit. It is a buying guide. For a user with complex posture needs, a physiotherapist or trained wheelchair service provider should do the assessment.

What makes a wheelchair the right choice for someone?

The right wheelchair meets the user's needs, suits the place where they spend most time, and fits their body with proper support. The WHO estimates that about 80 million people, or 1% of the world's population, are likely to need a wheelchair. Its training package describes eight steps of wheelchair service:

  1. Referral and appointment
  2. Assessment
  3. Prescription (selection)
  4. Funding and ordering
  5. Product preparation
  6. Fitting
  7. User training
  8. Maintenance, repairs and follow-up

When buying for a family member, you still go through most of these steps yourself, so the sections below follow the same order.

How do you measure someone for a wheelchair?

Take four body measurements with the person sitting upright, then convert each one into a wheelchair size. The WHO method is shown below.

Body measurement (mm)How to measureWheelchair size it gives
A. Hip widthAcross the hips or the widest part of the thighsSeat width = hip width
B. Seat depthBack of the pelvis to the back of the knee, both legsSeat depth = measurement less 30 to 60 mm (use the shorter leg)
C. Calf lengthBack of the knee to the base of the heel, ankle at 90 degrees, in everyday shoesTop of seat cushion to footrest (or to the floor for foot pushers)
D. Bottom of rib cageSeat to the bottom of the rib cageTop of seat cushion to top of backrest (active users)
E. Bottom of shoulder bladeSeat to the bottom of the shoulder blade, measured verticallyTop of seat cushion to top of backrest (users needing more support)

Practical tips from the WHO method:

  • Use a metal tape measure held straight, and read it at eye level.
  • Hold a clipboard or book against each hip, or behind the back, for a cleaner reading.
  • Empty the person's pockets first, and support their feet on the floor or on blocks.
  • Always measure both legs. If they differ, check the pelvis is level, then use the shorter side.
  • Allow for the thickness of the cushion the person will actually use.

How are wheelchair sizes described?

Wheelchair size is usually described by seat width, and sometimes by seat depth, with the seat height from the floor also useful to know. The WHO advises asking suppliers for the product specification, which lists measurements, weight, features and optional parts, and checking which parts adjust and over what range. Footrests, for example, usually adjust to several heights. Because sizes and adjustments vary by maker, send your measurements with your enquiry rather than only a seat width.

Which type of wheelchair suits which user?

Choose the frame by how the user moves, transfers and travels. The main frame options described by the WHO, plus attendant use, are:

How should a wheelchair fit when the user sits in it?

A good fit means the seat is close but not tight, the thighs and feet are fully supported, and the backrest suits the user's balance. The WHO fitting checks are:

  • Seat width: run your fingers between the outside of the thighs and the sides of the chair. They should fit comfortably without being pinched. Sides pressing on the thighs can cause a pressure sore, especially in users without normal feeling.
  • Seat depth: a two-finger gap (about 30 mm) between the back of the knee and the cushion. Up to 60 mm is acceptable for users with long legs. Check both sides.
  • Footrest height: the thighs rest evenly on the cushion with no gaps, and each foot is supported front and back. A gap under the thigh suggests the footrest is too high; a gap under the foot suggests it is too low.
  • Backrest height: up to the bottom of the rib cage suits a fit, active user with good balance. Up to the bottom of the shoulder blades suits a user who tires quickly or has difficulty sitting upright.
  • Rear wheel position: when the user holds the top of the push rim, the elbow should bend to about 90 degrees.

The WHO also advises checking the fit while the chair is still, then while the user pushes or is pushed.

Does every wheelchair need a cushion?

Yes. The WHO says every wheelchair user should have a cushion, and anyone at risk of pressure sores should have a pressure relief cushion. Solid seats should always have a cushion on top, and slung canvas seats can sag over time and stop giving support. The WHO describes two main pressure relief types: foam contoured cushions and air, fluid or gel filled cushions. Look for a cover that comes off for washing and resists water. For users who are incontinent, the WHO suggests a second cushion so one can dry while the other is in use. Our guide to equipment that helps prevent bed sores covers related products.

Which features make transfers and daily use easier?

Armrests, footrests and brakes make getting in and out easier, according to the WHO. Removable armrests, or armrests that follow the line of the rear wheels, help users who move sideways. Footrests that swing away help users who stand to transfer, and are essential for users who push with their feet. Brakes keep the chair still during every transfer. For daily life, a shorter frame is easier indoors, and high armrests can stop the user getting close to a table.

How do wheels and weight affect use on Pakistani streets and homes?

Larger wheels roll more easily over uneven ground, and wide rear wheels with large, wide castors stop the chair sinking into sand or mud, the WHO notes. That matters on broken pavements, unpaved lanes and gravel. A heavy chair takes more energy to push, whether by hand, foot or carer, and weight matters even more for children. Check doorway widths, bathroom access and any steps at home before you order.

What should you send when asking for a wheelchair quote?

Send enough detail for the supplier to match a model:

  • the four body measurements in millimetres, and the user's weight
  • how the chair will be pushed: by the user's arms, feet or a carer
  • where it will be used most: indoors, outdoors or both
  • whether it must fold for a car or rickshaw
  • whether a pressure relief cushion is needed

Frequently asked questions

How do I know what size wheelchair I need?

Measure hip width for seat width, back of pelvis to back of knee for seat depth (then take off 30 to 60 mm), heel to back of knee for footrest height, and seat to rib cage or shoulder blade for backrest height. This is the WHO method.

How much space should there be between the seat and the back of the knee?

The WHO fitting check is a two-finger gap of about 30 mm, with up to 60 mm acceptable for users with long legs.

Should a wheelchair seat be wider than the hips?

Only slightly. The WHO check is that your fingers fit between the thighs and the side of the chair without being pinched.

Is a folding or rigid wheelchair better?

A cross-folding chair is easier to transport and to fit through narrow doorways, while a rigid frame has fewer moving parts and can be more durable. Choose by how the chair will be stored and carried.

Do I need a cushion with a wheelchair?

Yes. The WHO says every wheelchair user should have a cushion, and users at risk of pressure sores need a pressure relief cushion.
