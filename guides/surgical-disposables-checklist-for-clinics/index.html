Surgical disposables checklist for clinics: what to order and how to specify it

Updated November 1, 2026 · 9 min read

Quick answer

A small clinic or OT usually needs gloves, masks, gowns, drapes, syringes, needles, IV cannulas, giving sets, catheters, gauze, sutures and specimen containers. For each item, specify the size or gauge, sterile or non-sterile, the standard it should meet, pack size and quantity. Clear specifications avoid wrong sizes and make quotes easy to compare.

Most ordering problems with disposables are not about price. They come from vague requests: "cannulas" without a gauge, "gloves" without a size split, "sutures" without a size or material. This checklist lists the common disposables for a small clinic or operation theatre and what to write down for each one, so any supplier can quote the right item.

How should a clinic use this checklist?

Start with what you used in the last three months, not with a catalogue. Note the item, the size or gauge, how many you used and whether it was sterile. Then add the details below for each line. Keep the final list as your standard order so every reorder is the same.

This is a buying checklist, not a clinical guide. Which item is used for which patient is a decision for your doctors and nurses and your facility's own policies.

What gloves and masks should be on the list?

Gloves: specify examination or sterile surgical, the material (nitrile, latex or vinyl), powder-free, the standard (for example ASTM D6319 for nitrile examination gloves or EN 455), the AQL and the quantity per size. Surgical gloves are usually listed by numbered size, examination gloves by letter size. Our nitrile vs latex vs vinyl gloves guide covers this in detail.

Masks: specify surgical mask or respirator, then the grade: EN 14683 Type I, II or IIR, ASTM F2100 Level 1, 2 or 3, or a respirator class such as N95 or FFP2. Add ear loops or ties. See surgical mask types explained.

What should you specify for gowns and drapes?

The US FDA separates surgical gowns, surgical isolation gowns and non-surgical isolation gowns. All surgical gowns must be supplied sterile and labelled as surgical gowns. The FDA also refers to the ANSI/AAMI PB70 system, which grades liquid barrier performance in four levels:

  • Level 1, minimal risk: for example basic care, standard isolation and visitor cover gowns.
  • Level 2, low risk: for example blood draws and suturing.
  • Level 3, moderate risk: for example arterial blood draws and IV insertion.
  • Level 4, high risk: for example long, fluid-intensive surgical procedures.

In Europe, surgical gowns and drapes are covered by EN 13795-1, which sets standard performance and high performance levels for critical and less critical product areas. For example, liquid penetration in critical areas must be at least 20 cm H2O for standard performance gowns and at least 100 cm H2O for high performance gowns and drapes, based on the 2019 edition. A newer edition was published in 2025, so ask which edition a report uses.

For gowns, specify: surgical or isolation, sterile or non-sterile, PB70 level or EN 13795 performance level, size and whether a towel is included. For drapes, specify: size, with or without a hole (fenestration) and its size, adhesive or plain, and EN 13795 performance level.

What should you specify for syringes and needles?

The ISO 7886 series covers sterile single-use hypodermic syringes. Part 3 covers auto-disable syringes, which are linked to fixed-dose immunisation, and Part 4 covers syringes with a reuse prevention feature. ISO 23908 covers sharps injury protection features. In 2015 the WHO called for a switch to safety-engineered syringes whenever appropriate.

For syringes, specify: volume (for example 1, 3, 5, 10 or 20 ml), Luer slip or Luer lock tip, with or without needle, the needle gauge and length if included, standard or safety type, and quantity per box.

For needles, specify gauge, length and pack size. Needle hubs are colour coded under ISO 6009, and a sterile single-use needle commonly cites ISO 7864. Our IV cannula and needle gauge guide lists the common colours.

What should you specify for IV cannulas and giving sets?

Peripheral IV cannulas are sized by gauge, and the smaller the gauge number the larger the diameter. NHS guidance lists common sizes from 24G (yellow) to 14G (orange) and notes that flow rates vary between manufacturers and are usually printed on the packaging. Specify gauge, length, ported or non-ported, safety or standard design, and quantity per gauge.

Gravity-feed IV giving sets (infusion sets) are covered by ISO 8536-4. Specify the drop factor printed on the set (drops per ml), total length, whether an air vent and an in-line filter are included, the type of connector, and whether you need a burette or extension set for some users.

What should you specify for catheters?

Urinary and many other catheters are sized in French (Fr or Ch). One French unit is one third of a millimetre, so the outer diameter in millimetres is the French size divided by three. A 12 Fr catheter is about 4 mm across.

Specify: catheter type (for example Foley, Nelaton or suction), French size, length (adult, female or paediatric), balloon volume for Foley catheters, material (latex or a latex-free material for users with latex allergy), number of ways for Foley catheters, and sterile single packs. Order collection bags separately with their volume and outlet type.

What should you specify for gauze and sutures?

Gauze and dressings: specify size in cm, number of ply, sterile or non-sterile, pieces per pack, and whether you need rolls, swabs or pads. State sterile or non-sterile for each use, since both are sold.

Sutures: suture thread size uses the USP scale, where more zeros mean a finer thread. A common reference table gives these approximate diameters:

5-0 and 4-00.1 and 0.15
3-0 and 2-00.2 and 0.3
0 and 10.35 and 0.4

For sutures, specify: USP size, material, absorbable or non-absorbable, thread length, needle shape and length, point type, and quantity per box. Copy these details from the box your surgeons already use.

What should you specify for specimen containers?

Specify: container type (urine, stool, sputum or general), volume in ml, sterile or non-sterile, screw cap, whether a label area or printed graduations are needed, and individual or bulk packing. If your laboratory sends samples outside, ask it which containers it accepts before ordering. Our laboratory equipment page covers related items.

Frequently asked questions

What disposables does a small clinic need most often?

Most clinics reorder gloves, masks, syringes, needles, IV cannulas, giving sets, gauze and specimen containers most often. OTs add sterile gloves, gowns, drapes, catheters and sutures.

How do I order IV cannulas correctly?

State the gauge, length, ported or non-ported, safety or standard design, and how many of each gauge. Gauge numbers run backwards: a 14G cannula is larger than a 24G cannula.

What does Fr mean on a catheter?

Fr stands for French, a size scale where one unit is one third of a millimetre. A 12 Fr catheter has an outer diameter of about 4 mm.

What is the difference between surgical and isolation gowns?

The US FDA requires surgical gowns to be sterile and labelled as surgical gowns for use in surgery. Isolation gowns are used for other levels of contamination risk and are graded by liquid barrier level under ANSI/AAMI PB70.

Should I buy safety syringes?

In 2015 the WHO called for a switch to safety-engineered syringes whenever appropriate. Standards such as ISO 7886-4 and ISO 23908 cover reuse prevention and sharps injury protection features, so name the type you need on your order.
