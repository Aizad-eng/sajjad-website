How to check blood pressure correctly at home

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

To check blood pressure at home, use a validated upper-arm monitor with the right cuff size. Avoid food, caffeine, smoking and exercise for 30 minutes, then sit with your back supported and feet flat for 5 minutes. Rest the cuffed bare arm at heart level, stay silent and take two readings 1 to 2 minutes apart.

Home blood pressure readings are only useful if they are taken the same careful way each time. A cuff that is too small, an arm hanging by your side or a cup of tea a few minutes before can all push the number up. This guide brings together the home measuring steps published by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the UK National Health Service (NHS), the British Heart Foundation and the charity Blood Pressure UK. It explains how to measure, not what your numbers mean for your health. Share your readings with your doctor and follow their advice.

What equipment do you need to check blood pressure at home?

You need a fully automatic upper-arm blood pressure monitor that has been clinically validated, a cuff that fits your arm and a way to record readings.

Blood Pressure UK says upper-arm monitors usually give the most accurate and consistent results, and recommends them over wrist or finger monitors. The British Heart Foundation also prefers an upper-arm monitor, with a wrist monitor as an alternative only if you cannot use an upper-arm cuff. Both point to the validated device lists kept by the British and Irish Hypertension Society, and the British Heart Foundation also names the STRIDE BP programme. Our guide on BP monitor validation explains how to check a model.

Blood Pressure UK adds that extra features are not necessary. A validated monitor plus a pen and paper is enough.

How do you choose the right cuff size?

Measure around the middle of your upper arm, halfway between the shoulder and the elbow, and pick a cuff whose printed range covers that number.

Blood Pressure UK warns that a cuff of the wrong size will not give a correct reading, and that most monitors come with a medium cuff, so you may need to order another size separately. Ranges differ by maker. As one example, Omron's home monitor accessory chart for Canada (updated December 2025) lists these cuffs:

What should you avoid before taking a reading?

For 30 minutes before a reading, avoid eating, caffeine, smoking, alcohol and exercise, and empty your bladder first.

The CDC lists all of these because they can raise the reading. Blood Pressure UK gives similar advice: do not measure within 30 minutes of caffeine or smoking, straight after exercise or alcohol, after a big meal, or when you need the toilet. Wear a short-sleeved or loose top so the sleeve does not squeeze the arm. The British Heart Foundation warns that a thick jumper or cardigan can affect the result.

How should you sit and position your arm?

Sit in a chair with your back supported and feet flat on the floor, and rest your arm on a table so the cuff is level with your heart.

  • Rest quietly for at least 5 minutes before measuring, as the CDC advises.
  • Keep your legs uncrossed.
  • Rest the arm on a table or desk. The CDC says a drooping arm can raise the reading. Blood Pressure UK suggests a cushion if needed to bring the cuff to heart height.
  • Keep your hand relaxed, palm up, and do not clench your fist.
  • Use the same arm every time. Blood Pressure UK suggests the arm your doctor or nurse uses.

How do you take the reading step by step?

Put the cuff on your bare upper arm, press start, stay still and silent until it finishes, then repeat after a short gap.

  1. Wrap the cuff on bare skin, not over clothing, following your monitor's instructions. The British Heart Foundation says the tube should run down the centre of the arm or slightly to the right.
  2. Check it is snug but not tight.
  3. Press start. Do not talk or move while the cuff inflates and deflates.
  4. If the cuff becomes uncomfortable, press stop to deflate it.
  5. Write down the reading.
  6. Wait 1 to 2 minutes and take a second reading. The CDC asks for at least two readings 1 to 2 minutes apart. The British Heart Foundation suggests a third if the first two differ.

Blood Pressure UK notes that the first reading is sometimes much higher than the next ones. If so, keep measuring until the readings settle.

How many readings should you take, and on how many days?

Measure at the same time each day, usually morning and evening, for as many days as your doctor asks. The published schedules differ slightly:

Blood Pressure UK also warns against checking too often, since worrying about small changes can itself raise your blood pressure.

How do you record and understand the two numbers?

Write down both numbers exactly as shown, with the date and time. The NHS explains that the top number (systolic) is the highest pressure when the heart pumps, and the bottom number (diastolic) is the lowest pressure when the heart rests between beats.

Blood Pressure UK asks you not to round readings up or down, so 127/84 stays 127/84. Use the monitor's memory, a notebook or a phone note, and take the log to your appointments, as the CDC advises. The NHS states that home readings are judged against a lower level than clinic readings, 135/85 at home compared with 140/90 in a clinic. Only your doctor can interpret your readings, so do not change any treatment based on them yourself. Blood Pressure UK says a single unexpected high reading may be nothing to worry about. Measure again at another time and see your doctor if readings stay high.

What common mistakes give a wrong reading?

The most common mistakes are a cuff of the wrong size, measuring over clothing, talking and an unsupported arm.

  • Cuff too small or too large for the arm.
  • Cuff over a sleeve or a thick jumper.
  • Talking, moving or feeling stressed during the reading.
  • Arm hanging down instead of resting at heart level.
  • Legs crossed or back unsupported.
  • Measuring soon after tea, coffee, a cigarette, a meal or exercise, or with a full bladder.
  • Switching arms between readings.

Blood Pressure UK adds that home monitoring is not suitable for people with an irregular pulse, such as atrial fibrillation, because it affects accuracy. Some monitors are designed for this, so talk to your doctor before buying.

How do you know your home monitor is still accurate?

Take your monitor to your next clinic visit so the nurse or doctor can compare it and watch how you use it, which Blood Pressure UK recommends. The British Heart Foundation advises servicing every two years, and Blood Pressure UK says this usually means sending the monitor back to the manufacturer. Frequently asked questions

What is the best time to check blood pressure at home?

Measure at the same time each day so readings compare like with like. The NHS and Blood Pressure UK suggest morning and evening, and your doctor may set a schedule.

Should I check blood pressure on the left or right arm?

Use the same arm every time, because each arm can read slightly differently. Blood Pressure UK suggests the arm your doctor or nurse uses.

Why is my first blood pressure reading higher?

Blood Pressure UK notes the first reading is often higher than the next ones. Take two or three readings a minute or two apart and keep going until they settle.

Can I check blood pressure over my sleeve?

No. The CDC says to place the cuff on your bare upper arm, and the British Heart Foundation warns that thick clothing can affect the result.

How long should I rest before measuring blood pressure?

The CDC advises sitting quietly with your back supported for at least 5 minutes before measuring, and avoiding food, caffeine, smoking, alcohol and exercise for 30 minutes.
