What is a manual (aneroid) BP apparatus?

A manual aneroid BP apparatus is a blood pressure device with a cuff, a rubber inflation bulb, a release valve, tubing and a dial gauge that turns cuff pressure into a reading using mechanical parts. The user pumps the cuff, lets the air out slowly and listens over the artery with a stethoscope for the Korotkoff sounds that mark the systolic and diastolic pressure. WHO describes three common formats: handheld (palm or pocket gauges), free-standing (on a stand) and wall-mounted units for examination rooms.

Because the reading depends on the person listening, the apparatus is normally bought together with a good stethoscope and used by staff who have been trained in the auscultatory method.

Is an aneroid BP apparatus still a good choice?

An aneroid BP apparatus is still useful where power is unreliable or a trained user wants a manual check, but WHO now prefers validated automated monitors for routine clinical use. In its 2020 technical specifications for automated blood pressure devices, WHO lists the aneroid's strengths as low cost, portability and no need for electricity. It also lists the weak points: observer error, the need for good hearing and steady deflation, and the fact that the gauge can lose accuracy when it is knocked.

WHO does not recommend aneroid devices for routine clinical measurement, because they need frequent recalibration and repeated training of staff. One practical option is a validated digital monitor as the main device, with an aneroid set kept as a backup. Our guide to digital BP apparatus prices in Pakistan covers the automated side.

Why are mercury BP apparatus being phased out?

Mercury BP apparatus are being phased out under the Minamata Convention on Mercury, which set 2020 as the date to stop manufacturing, importing and exporting mercury sphygmomanometers, with possible exemptions up to 2030. WHO and Health Care Without Harm launched a campaign in 2013 to make health care mercury-free by 2020 and asked countries to aim for that date. Aneroid and automated devices are the usual replacements. Check the current rule in Pakistan before buying or disposing of any mercury unit, and handle a broken mercury device with a proper spill procedure.

What cuff size do you need?

Choose the cuff by measuring around the middle of the upper arm and matching that number to the range printed on the cuff. A cuff that is too small or too large can give a wrong reading. Cuff guidance from Target: BP says the cuff bladder length should be at least 80 percent of the arm circumference and the width at least 37 percent.

Which standards apply to a manual sphygmomanometer?

The main international standard for manual devices is ISO 81060-1, which sets requirements and test methods for non-automated sphygmomanometers. WHO also lists BS EN 1060-2, which adds requirements for mechanical sphygmomanometers. Automated monitors are validated in clinical studies under a different standard, ISO 81060-2:2018.

WHO notes that most aneroid gauges cannot survive a fall from table height. The ISO and IEC working group therefore added drop requirements: apart from stationary units, devices must work normally after a 25 cm free fall, and a device labelled "shock resistant" must survive a 1 metre fall without losing performance. Welch Allyn's DuraShock range is one example of a gear-free gauge sold on shock resistance. Ask the seller which standard a model was tested to and whether the shock rating is stated in the manual.

How often should an aneroid gauge be checked for accuracy?

WHO says aneroid devices usually need an accuracy check and recalibration by technical experts every 6 months, or as the manufacturer recommends. American Diagnostic Corporation (ADC), a maker of aneroid gauges, advises a check against a reference at least every two years, and more often if the gauge is heavily used or has been dropped.

  • Zero check: with the cuff fully deflated, the needle should rest inside the small oval or box at zero. ADC says a needle outside that mark means the gauge is out of calibration and should not be used until it is serviced.
  • Reference check: WHO's test compares the gauge with a reference manometer at several points from 300 mmHg down to 0. A gauge within plus or minus 3 mmHg at every point meets the required accuracy.
  • Leaks: cuffs, tubing, bulbs and valves wear out. WHO's maintenance list includes replacing tubing, connectors and cuffs as needed.

A needle at zero does not prove the gauge is accurate across the scale. Our guide on how to check if a BP apparatus is accurate explains the steps in more detail.

What should you check before ordering a manual BP apparatus?

  1. The gauge format you need: palm, cuff-mounted, desk or wall.
  2. The cuff sizes, based on the arm sizes of the people you see.
  3. Whether the gauge is described as shock resistant, and the drop height stated.
  4. The manual, including the accuracy claim and the recommended check interval.
  5. Availability of spare bulbs, valves, tubing and cuffs.
  6. Whether a stethoscope is included, and its quality.

Frequently asked questions

Is a manual BP apparatus more accurate than a digital one?

Not automatically. WHO prefers validated automated monitors for routine clinical use, because aneroid readings depend on the user's technique and the gauge can drift out of accuracy when knocked.

How often should an aneroid BP gauge be checked?

WHO says aneroid devices usually need checking by technical experts every 6 months or as the manufacturer recommends. ADC advises at least every two years, and sooner if the gauge is heavily used or dropped.

What does it mean if the needle is outside the zero mark?

ADC says a gauge whose needle rests outside the zero oval when fully deflated is out of calibration and should not be used until it has been serviced.

Can I still buy a mercury BP apparatus?

The Minamata Convention set 2020 as the phase-out date for making, importing and exporting mercury sphygmomanometers, with possible exemptions to 2030. Check the current rule in Pakistan before buying one.

Which cuff size should I order for a clinic?

Measure around the middle of the upper arm and match it to the cuff range. Under the Target: BP chart, adult cuffs cover 27 to 34 cm and large adult cuffs cover 35 to 44 cm, so most clinics stock both.
