Pulse oximeter for babies and children in Pakistan: sensor sizes, types and price factors

Quick answer

Parents, paediatric clinics and children's wards often ask for a pulse oximeter that works on small hands and feet. The key point is size: a sensor made for an adult finger does not sit correctly on a baby, and that can give a misleading number. This guide explains the types of oximeter used for children, how sensors are sized by weight, what affects the reading and what changes the price in Pakistan. It is a buying guide only. Decisions about a child's oxygen levels belong with a doctor.

Can you use an adult pulse oximeter on a baby or child?

You should not rely on an adult oximeter probe for a baby or young child, because probes for babies and children must be chosen by the child's weight. In a 2018 patient safety alert, NHS England warned that putting an adult probe on a baby or child, or a finger probe on an ear, can give a reading up to 50 percent lower or 30 percent higher than the true value. A falsely high number can reassure carers when a child is getting worse, and a falsely low one can lead to an unneeded intervention.

The same alert asked hospitals to keep the full range of probes needed for babies and children wherever oximetry is used.

What types of pulse oximeter are used for babies and children?

There are three broad types: monitors with cable sensors in neonatal, infant and paediatric sizes, fingertip oximeters, and wearable sock-style monitors for infants.

Which sensor size fits which child?

Sensor size is chosen by body weight, as printed on the sensor pack. Masimo, an oximetry maker, publishes these weight ranges for its single-patient-use LNCS sensors:

Masimo LNCS sensorPatient weight range
NeoPt (preterm)Under 1 kg
Neo (neonatal)Under 3 kg, or over 40 kg
Inf (infant)3 to 20 kg
Pdtx (paediatric)10 to 50 kg
Adtx (adult)Over 30 kg

The ranges overlap, so a 15 kg child could fit an infant or a paediatric sensor; the clinical team decides which site and sensor suit the child. Other brands use their own size names and ranges, so always read the pack, and give the monitor brand and model when you order so the sensor connector can be matched.

What affects the accuracy of an oximeter reading in children?

The wrong sensor size or placement is one of the biggest causes of error, and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lists other factors too. On its pulse oximeter page, the FDA names poor circulation, skin pigmentation, skin thickness, skin temperature, tobacco use and fingernail polish as things that can affect accuracy.

The FDA says that in 2022 it funded a real-world study of oximeter accuracy in children with academic partners. In January 2025 it issued draft guidance on how oximeters for medical purposes should be tested and labelled; when we checked the page at the time of writing (November 2026), that draft was the latest step it listed. Our guides on pulse oximeter accuracy and how to read a pulse oximeter go into more detail.

Should you use a baby oximeter to watch a baby's sleep at home?

Do not buy a home oximeter as a way to prevent sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). The American Academy of Pediatrics advises parents not to use home cardiorespiratory monitors to reduce SIDS risk. Its parent site, HealthyChildren.org, adds that consumer heart rate and oximetry monitors, including wearables, have no evidence of lowering SIDS risk and may give false reassurance.

Some infant devices have gone through regulatory review for other purposes. In November 2023 Owlet announced FDA De Novo clearance of its Dream Sock as an over-the-counter oximeter for infants aged 1 to 18 months and weighing 6 to 30 lb, and its BabySat is a prescription device for infants with medical conditions. If a doctor has asked you to monitor a child at home, follow your doctor's advice on the device to use.

Should you buy reusable or single-patient-use sensors?

The choice depends on how many children you monitor, for how long, and on your hospital's infection control rules. NHS England notes that oximeter probes can be single-use or multiple-use and are designed to attach to specific parts of the body. Masimo's LNCS range, for example, is sold as single-patient-use adhesive sensors in each weight band.

The NHS alert also found that once a probe is out of its packaging, there is often nothing on it to remind staff where it should go. It asked makers to improve labelling and asked hospitals to put reminders at the point of use. A simple step when you receive stock is to keep each size in a clearly labelled box, with the weight range written on the front, so the right sensor is picked in a hurry.

What should you check before ordering a pulse oximeter for children?

  1. The child's weight, and the sensor weight range printed on the pack.
  2. For hospitals, the monitor brand and model so the sensor connector matches.
  3. Whether you need reusable sensors, single-patient-use sensors or both.
  4. For fingertip units, the stated age or finger-size range in the manual.
  5. The manual's accuracy figure and the conditions it was measured in.
  6. Regulatory status, and whether a wearable's app works in Pakistan.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use my own fingertip oximeter on my baby?

Only if its manual says it suits a child of that size. NHS England warns that using an adult probe on a baby or child can give a reading far from the true value, so use a sensor sized for the child's weight.

How are baby oximeter sensors sized?

By body weight. Masimo, for example, lists its LNCS infant sensor for 3 to 20 kg and its paediatric sensor for 10 to 50 kg.

Does a baby oximeter prevent SIDS?

No. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against using home cardiorespiratory monitors to reduce SIDS risk and says consumer oximetry monitors have no evidence of lowering it.

What can make a child's oximeter reading wrong?

The wrong sensor size or placement, poor circulation, skin temperature and nail polish can all affect the reading. The FDA also lists skin pigmentation and skin thickness.
