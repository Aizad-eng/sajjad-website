KN95 vs N95 vs FFP2 respirators: how the three standards compare

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

KN95, N95 and FFP2 are national ratings for disposable respirators. N95 is approved by the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) under 42 CFR Part 84, KN95 follows China's GB 2626 and FFP2 follows Europe's EN 149. N95 and KN95 require at least 95 percent filtration of a salt test aerosol and FFP2 at least 94 percent.

KN95, N95 and FFP2 respirators look alike and are often sold side by side, which makes ordering confusing. Each name comes from a different country's standard, with its own tests and labels. This guide compares the three on the numbers that matter, explains what changed in China's GB 2626-2019, and lists what to check on the label and in the paperwork before you place an order. It covers equipment only. Which respirator staff should wear for a task is a decision for your facility's infection control team.

What are KN95, N95 and FFP2 respirators?

They are disposable filtering facepiece respirators rated under three national standards: N95 in the United States, KN95 in China and FFP2 in Europe.

  • N95: approved by the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) under 42 CFR Part 84.
  • KN95: a class in China's mandatory standard GB 2626 for non-powered air-purifying particle respirators.
  • FFP2: a class in the European standard EN 149 for filtering half masks that protect against particles.

All three are tight-fitting respirators. They are different from surgical masks, which are loose barriers against splashes. Our guide to surgical masks vs N95 respirators explains that difference.

How do KN95, N95 and FFP2 compare on filtration?

On paper they are close: N95 and KN95 require at least 95 percent filtration of a salt test aerosol, and FFP2 requires at least 94 percent.

The World Health Organization (WHO) technical specification of 7 August 2020 puts the three side by side. The table below uses that document plus the US regulation and the French safety institute INRS.

Is a KN95 the same as an N95?

No. A KN95 can meet similar filtration numbers, but it is approved under a different standard and is not a NIOSH approved N95.

Only NIOSH can approve an N95. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says a NIOSH approved respirator must carry a TC approval number (TC-84A-XXXX), the word NIOSH in capital letters or its logo, the approval holder's name, the filter rating and a model number. A KN95 will not carry a NIOSH approval unless that exact model has also been approved by NIOSH. The test methods also differ. Under 42 CFR 84.174, N-series filters are tested with a salt aerosol at 85 litres per minute after 25 hours at high humidity. EN 149 measures filtration at 95 litres per minute and adds a paraffin oil test.

What do FFP1, FFP2 and FFP3 mean?

They are the three EN 149 classes, and the number rises with filtration and lower allowed leakage.

INRS gives the minimum filtration as 80 percent for FFP1, 94 percent for FFP2 and 99 percent for FFP3. Total inward leakage, measured on people in the lab, must be below 22 percent, 8 percent and 2 percent. EN 149 respirators may also carry letters after the class. "NR" means single shift only, "R" means reusable, and "D" means it passed an optional dolomite dust clogging test. For example, FFP2 NR D.

What do the KN and KP letters mean, and what changed in GB 2626-2019?

KN classes are for non-oily particles and KP classes are for oily particles, and the 2019 edition of GB 2626 tightened breathing resistance rules.

A Bureau Veritas bulletin of June 2020 explains that GB 2626-2019 replaced GB 2626-2006. Its start date was moved from 1 July 2020 to 1 July 2021, and until then makers could use either edition. The 2006 edition had one breathing resistance limit for all masks. The 2019 edition sets limits by class and type. For a disposable KN95 without a valve, the inhalation limit is 210 Pa. Masks that fail the flammability test must now carry a warning not to use them near open flames or sparks. Ask which edition a KN95 was tested to.

Which of the three is used in health care?

All three appear in WHO health care purchase specifications, but the fluid-resistant versions are listed separately.

The WHO specification lists N95, FFP2 or KN95 (or an equivalent) as the minimum for a non-fluid-resistant respirator. For a fluid-resistant respirator it lists a NIOSH approved surgical N95, an FFP2 that also meets EN 14683 Type IIR, or a Chinese GB 19083 Grade 1 medical protective mask. If your theatre or ward needs splash protection as well as filtration, write that on the order.

Do KN95, N95 and FFP2 respirators need fit testing?

Yes, all three are tight-fitting, so they only protect as rated when they seal well.

INRS stresses that leaks around the face make the protection ineffective. The US workplace rule 29 CFR 1910.134 asks for a fit test before first use, when changing size, model or make, and at least once a year. It also requires a user seal check each time the respirator is put on, and bars tight-fitting facepieces where facial hair crosses the seal. Follow your own facility's policy in Pakistan.

How do you check the label on each type?

Each type has its own markings, and the paperwork should match them.

  • N95: check the CDC label items, then search the TC number on the NIOSH Certified Equipment List and match the model.
  • FFP2: INRS lists the CE marking, the standard number and year (EN 149) and the class as required markings. INRS also warns that medical masks carry a CE mark under the medical device rules, which is a different thing.
  • KN95: look for the GB 2626 number and edition year, then ask for the test report for that exact model.

For all three, the WHO purchase checklist asks for test reports from an ISO 17025 accredited lab. Our guides on CE mark vs FDA vs ISO 13485 and verifying a supplier on Alibaba help with the paperwork.

What should you write on a respirator order?

Name the standard and class, and state anything else your facility needs.

  • Type and standard: N95 (NIOSH), KN95 (GB 2626-2019) or FFP2 (EN 149).
  • Fluid resistance needed or not (surgical N95, or FFP2 with Type IIR).
  • Valve or no valve.
  • Headbands or ear loops, and size if offered.
  • Brand and model number, and the TC number for N95s.
  • Pieces per box and number of boxes.

Frequently asked questions

Is KN95 as good as N95?

On paper both require at least 95 percent filtration of a salt test aerosol, but they are tested and approved under different standards. A KN95 is not a NIOSH approved N95 unless that exact model holds a NIOSH approval.

Is FFP2 the same as N95?

They are close but not identical. FFP2 requires at least 94 percent filtration under EN 149 and includes a paraffin oil test, while N95 requires at least 95 percent under NIOSH 42 CFR Part 84.

What is the difference between FFP2 and FFP3?

FFP3 filters at least 99 percent and allows under 2 percent total inward leakage, while FFP2 filters at least 94 percent and allows under 8 percent, according to INRS.

What does KP95 mean?

KP classes in GB 2626 are for oily particles, while KN classes are for non-oily particles. KP95 is the oily-particle class at the 95 level.

Which GB 2626 edition should a KN95 be tested to?

GB 2626-2019 replaced the 2006 edition, with its start date moved to 1 July 2021. Ask the supplier which edition the test report covers.
