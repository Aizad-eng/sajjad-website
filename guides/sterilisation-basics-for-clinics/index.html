Sterilisation basics for small clinics: equipment and process overview

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

Sterilisation in a small clinic means cleaning reusable instruments, checking and packing them, running them through a steam steriliser (autoclave) and storing them so they stay sterile. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) both name steam under pressure as the preferred method for heat-tolerant instruments.

This guide gives clinic owners and buyers an overview of how reusable instruments are made safe again, and which equipment and consumables that takes. It summarises published guidance from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). It is not a sterilisation protocol. Your clinic should follow national rules, a written infection control policy and each manufacturer's instructions.

Which items need sterilisation and which only need disinfection?

Items that enter sterile tissue or blood need sterilisation, while items that only touch skin usually need cleaning and low-level disinfection. CDC guidance uses the Spaulding classification, which sorts items by infection risk:

CategoryWhat the item touchesExamplesMinimum processing
CriticalSterile tissue or the bloodstreamSurgical instruments, implants, dental forceps and elevatorsSterilisation
SemicriticalMucous membranes or non-intact skinLaryngoscope blades, mouth mirrors, some endoscopesHigh-level disinfection at minimum; heat sterilisation if the item tolerates it
NoncriticalIntact skin onlyBlood pressure cuffs, bedpans, crutchesCleaning and low-level disinfection

For dental settings, CDC adds that most semicritical items are heat tolerant and should also be heat sterilised. It says dental handpieces should always be heat sterilised between patients.

What are the steps in the decontamination cycle?

Reprocessing is a loop of cleaning, disinfection, checking, packing, sterilisation, storage and use, and every step matters. The WHO manual on decontamination (2016) shows this life cycle and stresses that an error at any stage can harm patients and staff. In a small clinic the cycle usually looks like this:

  1. Pre-soak or rinse used instruments so blood and tissue do not dry on them.
  2. Clean them, by hand, in an ultrasonic cleaner or in a washer-disinfector.
  3. Rinse, dry and check each item for remaining soil and damage.
  4. Open hinged instruments, take apart items with removable parts, and pack them in pouches, wraps or containers.
  5. Run a validated steriliser cycle and record it.
  6. Cool, check the indicators, then store in a clean, closed space.

Why does cleaning have to come before sterilisation?

Cleaning comes first because dried blood, saliva and tissue can shield microorganisms from steam. CDC states that residue left on instruments acts as a barrier to the sterilant, and that cleaning should happen as soon as possible after use. WHO repeats that cleaning always precedes sterilisation.

For equipment, CDC's dental summary recommends automated cleaning (an ultrasonic cleaner or a washer-disinfector) where possible, to improve cleaning and reduce staff exposure to blood. WHO advises long-handled brushes, heavy household gloves and eye protection for manual cleaning, and says the detergent in an ultrasonic cleaner should be changed at least at the end of each clinical session.

What temperatures and times do steam sterilisers use?

Steam sterilisers commonly run at 121 °C or 132 to 135 °C, with the holding time set by the steriliser type and load. Both WHO and CDC name steam under pressure as the preferred method for heat-tolerant critical items. CDC lists four parameters for steam: steam quality, pressure, temperature and time.

MethodTemperatureMinimum exposure timeSource
Gravity displacement steam, wrapped items121 °C30 minutesCDC
Prevacuum steam, wrapped items132 °C4 minutesCDC
Steam flush pressure pulse132 to 135 °C3 to 4 minutesCDC
Bowie-Dick air removal test cycle134 °C3.5 minutesCDC
Dry heat oven170 °C or 150 °C60 minutes or 150 minutesWHO

These are reference figures, not settings to copy. Drying time is extra, and items with lumens, textiles or heavy metal trays may need longer cycles. Always use the cycles the steriliser maker has validated for your type of load. WHO lists dry heat as not recommended as best practice for medical devices, and limits it to items that moisture would damage.

Which type of steriliser suits a small clinic?

Most small clinics use a tabletop steam steriliser, often called an autoclave. WHO describes the tabletop model as the steriliser most often used in outpatient, dental and rural clinics: a chamber of no more than two cubic feet that makes its own steam from distilled or deionised water. WHO notes these units are designed for small instruments, such as dental instruments, and does not recommend them for instruments with lumens.

The cycle type matters as much as the size. Small sterilisers built to the European standard EN 13060 run class N, S or B cycles, and only some of these are designed for wrapped or hollow items. Our autoclave buying guide explains the differences.

Which methods do not sterilise instruments?

Boiling, ultraviolet (UV) light, microwaves and glass bead sterilisers do not reliably sterilise instruments. The WHO manual lists all four as unacceptable sterilisation methods. In its dental section it states that UV light and boiling water do not sterilise instruments and must not be used, and that dry heat and chemicals are not recommended for routine sterilisation of dental instruments.

Fast unwrapped "flash" cycles are also discouraged for routine use. WHO explains that instruments come out wet, hot and unpackaged, which raises the chance of recontamination.

How do you know a sterilisation cycle worked?

You confirm a cycle with three kinds of monitoring: mechanical, chemical and biological. CDC and WHO describe them as follows:

  • Mechanical: read the gauges, display or printout for time, temperature and pressure on every cycle, and record them.
  • Chemical: place an indicator inside every package, plus an external indicator if the inside one cannot be seen. ISO 11140 sorts chemical indicators into six types, from simple process indicators to integrating and emulating indicators.
  • Biological (spore tests): CDC recommends at least weekly testing, and daily testing if the steriliser runs several loads a day.
  • Air removal test: vacuum-type sterilisers need a daily Bowie-Dick test in an empty chamber before the first load.

Mechanical and chemical results do not prove sterility on their own, but they catch errors quickly. If an indicator has not changed colour, the items should not be used.

How should sterilised instruments be packed, labelled and stored?

Pack instruments so steam can reach every surface, label every pack, and store packs dry and closed. CDC's dental summary says packages should show the steriliser used, the cycle or load number, the sterilisation date and any expiry date, so items can be recalled after a failure.

Load items with space between them, pouches on their edge and containers placed so water drains. Packs that come out wet are considered contaminated. Many hospitals now use event-related shelf life: a pack stays sterile until something damages it, such as a tear, moisture or a broken seal. CDC suggests storing packs 8 to 10 inches above the floor, 5 inches below the ceiling and 2 inches from outside walls, and never under sinks. Any pack found wet, torn or punctured should be cleaned, repacked and sterilised again.

What equipment and consumables does a small clinic need?

A small clinic needs a cleaning area, a suitable steam steriliser, packing materials, indicators and closed storage. A typical buying list:

  • Deep sink, long-handled brushes, heavy-duty gloves, eye protection and an apron.
  • Ultrasonic cleaner or washer-disinfector, with a suitable detergent.
  • Tabletop steam steriliser with a cycle type that matches your loads.
  • Distilled or deionised water, if the steriliser maker requires it.
  • Sterilisation pouches or wrap, and a heat sealer if your pouches need one.
  • Internal and external chemical indicators, spore tests, and Bowie-Dick or helix tests for vacuum sterilisers.
  • Cycle log book or printer, and closed cabinets for sterile storage.

Frequently asked questions

Can boiling water sterilise surgical instruments?

No. The WHO manual lists boiling as an unacceptable sterilisation method and states that boiling water does not sterilise instruments.

What temperature does an autoclave use?

Common steam cycles run at 121 °C or about 132 to 135 °C. CDC lists minimum exposure times of 30 minutes at 121 °C in gravity sterilisers and 4 minutes at 132 °C in prevacuum sterilisers for wrapped items, but always use the maker's validated cycles.

How often should a clinic do a spore test?

CDC recommends biological spore testing of sterilisers at least weekly, and daily if the steriliser runs several loads a day. Follow your national guidance if it is stricter.

Do instruments need cleaning before going in the autoclave?

Yes. Both WHO and CDC say cleaning must come before sterilisation, because dried blood and tissue can shield microorganisms from steam.

How long do sterilised instrument packs stay sterile?

Many facilities use event-related shelf life, where a pack stays sterile until it is torn, wet or its seal is broken. Store packs in clean, closed cabinets and reprocess any damaged pack.
