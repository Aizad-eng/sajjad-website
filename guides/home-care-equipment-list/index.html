Home care equipment list after a hospital stay: what families usually arrange

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

A home care equipment list after a hospital stay usually covers mobility aids such as walkers and wheelchairs, a hospital bed and pressure mattress, toilet aids such as a commode chair, home monitors such as a BP monitor or glucometer, and prescribed items such as oxygen. The hospital care team decides which items each patient actually needs.

Coming home after a hospital stay often means the house has to change quickly. This guide lists the equipment types that commonly come up, explains who should decide, and gives a checklist for getting the home ready. It does not tell you what any patient needs. That decision belongs to the doctors, nurses, physiotherapists and occupational therapists treating them.

Who decides what equipment is needed after discharge?

The care team treating the patient decides, based on an assessment of how the person will manage at home. In the UK, the NHS says hospital staff should make sure a patient going home has any new equipment they need and knows how to use it. Age UK describes a recovery and support plan agreed before discharge, which can include equipment, and says home adaptations should be ready before the person is sent home. At North Bristol NHS Trust, hospital occupational therapists assess patients for equipment such as commodes, perching stools, chair and bed raisers and hoists.

Before you buy anything, ask the treating team three questions: which items are needed, for how long, and whether any item needs a prescription or specific settings.

What are the main categories of home care equipment?

Most home care equipment falls into six groups: mobility, bed and transfer, toilet and bathroom, monitoring, breathing support and day-to-day supplies. The US Medicare programme gives a useful picture of what counts as durable home equipment. Its list includes canes, crutches, walkers, wheelchairs, commode chairs, hospital beds, oxygen equipment, CPAP machines, infusion pumps and blood sugar monitors with their strips and lancets. Medicare defines durable equipment as items that withstand repeated use, are used for a medical reason in the home and are expected to last at least 3 years.

What mobility equipment might be needed at home?

Mobility equipment ranges from a walking stick or walker for someone who can stand, to a wheelchair for someone who cannot walk safely. The right choice depends on strength, balance and the layout of the home, which is why a physiotherapist's view matters. North Bristol NHS Trust separates short-term wheelchair use (under 6 months) from long-term use, which goes through a specialist referral with its own criteria. That is a useful way to think about it: a short recovery may suit a simple folding chair, while long-term use needs proper sizing. Our wheelchair page covers the common types.

What bed and transfer equipment is used at home?

A hospital bed, a pressure-redistributing mattress and transfer aids are the usual items for someone who spends long hours in bed. A hospital bed lets carers raise the head or the whole bed, which can make care and transfers easier. Medicare lists hospital beds as home medical equipment. The NHS advises specially designed mattresses and cushions for people at risk of pressure ulcers, so ask the nurse whether one is needed. Our guide to bed sore prevention equipment explains the mattress types, and the hospital bed page covers manual and electric beds.

Hoists and bed raisers also appear on NHS equipment lists. Measure the room, the doorway and the height of the existing bed before ordering.

What toilet and bathroom aids help after a hospital stay?

Commode chairs, perching stools and other toileting aids help people who cannot safely walk to or use a standard toilet. Commodes appear on both the NHS trust equipment list and the Medicare list. When choosing one, check the seat height, the weight limit stated by the maker, whether the arms drop down for side transfers, and whether it fits beside the bed or in the bathroom. Ask the occupational therapist or nurse which type suits the patient's transfers.

Which monitoring devices are commonly kept at home?

A BP monitor, a thermometer, a pulse oximeter and a glucometer are the monitors most often kept at home, but only buy the ones the doctor asks you to use. Medicare lists blood sugar monitors, test strips, control solutions and lancets as home supplies, which reminds buyers that a glucometer is only useful with matching strips. Write down what the doctor wants recorded, how often, and at what values to call them. Follow your doctor's advice.

What about oxygen and breathing equipment?

Oxygen and most breathing equipment should only be used when a doctor has prescribed it. The NHS warns that using oxygen bought online without a prescription can damage the brain, heart and lungs or even cause death. It describes a home oxygen concentrator as about the size of a bedside table, plugged into a normal socket. Nebulizers, CPAP machines and suction machines also need settings and training from the care team. Our guide on how to choose an oxygen concentrator explains flow, purity, alarms and power.

Which disposables and supplies should be on the list?

The nurse or doctor will tell you which supplies are needed, and these often run out faster than families expect. Typical items include examination gloves, gauze and dressings, tape, syringes, urine bags, catheters, oxygen tubing or nasal cannulas, and wipes. Ask for exact sizes (for example catheter size or syringe volume), how many per day, and whether items must be sterile. Keep a written list so that reorders are easy.

What should you check before the equipment arrives home?

Check that the home, the power supply and the carers are ready for each device. The US FDA home healthcare checklist suggests asking whether a device needs electricity, running water or other connections, and whether stairs, doorways, bathrooms or house wiring could cause problems. It advises keeping the instructions next to the device, learning what alarms and error messages mean, asking for a hands-on demonstration, keeping spare batteries where suitable, and having a backup plan if the device fails.

Power cuts need a plan. The FDA advises asking the manufacturer whether a device can run on batteries or a generator, never plugging in a wet cord or device, and checking that settings have not changed after power returns.

  • Get the equipment list in writing from the care team.
  • Measure doors, rooms and bed height.
  • Confirm the power needs and the backup for anything electric.
  • Ask for a demonstration and keep the manuals.
  • Note the phone numbers for the doctor and the supplier.

Frequently asked questions

What equipment do you need at home after a hospital stay?

It depends on the patient. Common items are a walker or wheelchair, a hospital bed and pressure mattress, a commode chair, home monitors and any prescribed oxygen or breathing equipment. The care team should give you the list.

Who decides which home care equipment a patient needs?

The doctors, nurses, physiotherapists and occupational therapists treating the patient. NHS guidance says staff should make sure patients have the equipment they need and know how to use it before going home.

Should I buy an oxygen concentrator before discharge just in case?

No. Oxygen should only be used on prescription. The NHS warns that unprescribed oxygen can damage the brain, heart and lungs.

What should I check before electric equipment arrives at home?

Check the power supply, whether the device can run on a battery or generator during power cuts, the room space and door widths. Ask for a demonstration and keep the manual next to the device.

Which home monitors are most useful after hospital?

Only the ones the doctor asks you to use, usually a BP monitor, thermometer, pulse oximeter or glucometer. Ask what to record, how often and when to call.
