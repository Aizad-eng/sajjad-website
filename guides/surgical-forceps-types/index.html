Surgical forceps types: tissue, dressing and artery forceps explained

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

Surgical forceps fall into two groups: non-locking thumb forceps, such as toothed tissue forceps and smooth dressing forceps, and locking forceps with a ratchet, such as artery forceps (haemostats) and Allis forceps. Tissue forceps hold skin and tissue, dressing forceps handle gauze and sutures, and artery forceps clamp bleeding vessels.

A typical instrument catalogue lists several artery forceps and dissecting forceps patterns that look alike. This guide explains the main surgical forceps types, the difference between toothed and non-toothed tips, the common artery forceps patterns and what to write when you order. It covers equipment only, not clinical technique.

What are the main types of surgical forceps?

Surgical forceps are either non-locking or locking. Non-locking forceps, often called thumb forceps or pick-ups, are held in a pen grip and spring open when released. Locking forceps, sometimes called clamps, are hinged in the middle like scissors and use a ratchet on the handles to hold the jaws closed. The ratchet interlocks when closed and releases with a shearing push of the handles.

The way each type is held explains its shape. WHO describes holding tissue forceps like a pen. For artery forceps, it describes placing the thumb and fingers through the handles only as far as needed for control, with the index finger on the shaft to give three points of contact. Handle length and ring size therefore matter as much as the jaws, so ask for the overall length in centimetres for every pattern you order.

What is the difference between toothed and non-toothed forceps?

Toothed forceps have small interlocking teeth at the tip to hold tissue without slipping, while non-toothed forceps have smooth or cross-hatched tips for handling dressings, swabs and sutures.

The teeth come in set patterns such as 1 x 2, 7 x 7 and 9 x 9. Because teeth grip with less overall pressure, they can crush tissue less than smooth tips. The World Health Organization (WHO) manual Surgical Care at the District Hospital makes the same point, noting that toothed forceps are less likely to crush tissue.

Teeth are still not harmless. A University of Bristol clinical skills sheet notes that rat tooth forceps cause a degree of tissue damage and should not be used on delicate tissue. What are dressing forceps used for?

Dressing forceps were originally designed for handling gauze. The University of Bristol sheet lists other uses: holding gauze, pulling out dead or infected tissue during wound cleaning, removing foreign material from a wound, pulling back skin to see an injury, and handling sutures.

Dressing forceps are a core part of a dressing set, alongside scissors and a tray. Our dressing set guide lists the full contents.

What are Adson and DeBakey forceps?

Adson and DeBakey are two named patterns of non-locking tissue forceps.

  • Adson forceps: short, fine tissue forceps. The toothed version has a 1 x 2 tooth pattern at the tip. Sterile minor operations packs from UK suppliers list Adson forceps of 12.5 cm in both toothed and non-toothed versions.
  • DeBakey forceps: atraumatic forceps used widely in cardiothoracic, vascular, and head and neck surgery. They are named after the American cardiovascular surgeon Michael DeBakey (1908 to 2008).

What are artery forceps (haemostats)?

Artery forceps, also called haemostats or haemostatic clamps, are locking forceps that control bleeding by clamping exposed blood vessels shut. They are also known as Pean forceps, after the surgeon Jules-Emile Pean, who is credited with designing the modern haemostat.

The handles lock using a few interlocking teeth on each handle, which lets the user choose how tightly the jaws close. When locked, the tips press with roughly 40 newtons of force. The University of Bristol sheet adds that artery forceps come in many sizes and varieties and should not be used as needle holders. If your team needs to grip needles, order a proper needle holder; see our needle holder types guide.

Which artery forceps patterns are most common?

Common named artery forceps patterns include Spencer Wells, Kelly, Crile, Halsted mosquito, Kocher and Rankin, each available with straight or curved tips.

  • Spencer Wells: Thomas Spencer Wells introduced an improved artery forceps pattern in 1879 that stopped the handles catching surrounding structures. It is often described as the forerunner of today's artery forceps.
  • Halsted mosquito: minor operations packs list a curved 12.5 cm Halsted mosquito.
  • Kelly and Crile: other named haemostat patterns. Kelly forceps are named after Howard Atwood Kelly.
  • Specialist clamps: Mixter right-angle forceps and Satinsky clamps are also listed among haemostat types.

Patterns differ in length and jaw shape, so ask for the catalogue drawing or a clear photo of the jaws before you order a pattern you have not used before.

What are Allis forceps used for?

Allis forceps are locking forceps with sharp teeth used to grasp heavy tissue such as fascia, and soft tissue such as breast or bowel. Because the teeth can damage tissue, they are often used on tissue that is about to be removed. The American surgeon Oscar Allis invented them in 1883.

Which standards apply to surgical forceps?

ISO 7151 is the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standard that sets general requirements and test methods for non-cutting articulated (jointed) surgical instruments. That description fits ratcheted forceps such as artery forceps, but ask the supplier which standard each item is actually made to.

The edition listed at the time of writing (November 2026) is ISO 7151:2024, published in April 2024, which follows the 1988 edition. ISO 7153-1:2016 lists the metals commonly used for standard surgical instruments. Ask your supplier which standard and steel grade apply.

What should you check and specify when ordering forceps?

Specify the pattern name, length, straight or curved, and toothed or non-toothed, then check the tips and ratchet when the forceps arrive.

  • Pattern and length: for example Adson toothed 12.5 cm, or Halsted mosquito curved 12.5 cm.
  • Tip type: toothed, non-toothed, atraumatic or the tooth pattern (1 x 2, 7 x 7 and so on).
  • Quantity per set: most sets need at least one toothed and one non-toothed pair.
  • Tip alignment: WHO warns that forceps that cross at the tip are frustrating and dangerous. Close each pair and look at the tips.
  • Ratchet action: on locking forceps, every ratchet step should hold and release smoothly.
  • Reprocessing: WHO advises sterilising all surgical instruments after use, so reusable forceps must stand up to repeated cleaning and sterilising. Ask which steel is used and whether the maker supplies reprocessing instructions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between tissue forceps and dressing forceps?

Tissue forceps usually have teeth to hold skin and tissue without slipping. Dressing forceps have smooth or cross-hatched tips for handling gauze, dressings and sutures.

Are toothed forceps more traumatic than non-toothed forceps?

Not always. Teeth grip with less pressure, and WHO notes toothed forceps are less likely to crush tissue, but rat tooth forceps still cause some damage and are not meant for delicate tissue.

What is the difference between artery forceps and a needle holder?

Artery forceps clamp bleeding vessels, while a needle holder is built to grip a suture needle firmly. A University of Bristol clinical skills sheet states that artery forceps should not be used as needle holders.

What are mosquito forceps?

Mosquito forceps, such as the Halsted mosquito, are a pattern of locking artery forceps. Minor operations packs commonly include a curved 12.5 cm version.

Which ISO standard covers artery forceps?

ISO 7151 covers non-cutting articulated (jointed) surgical instruments, which describes artery forceps. The edition listed at the time of writing is ISO 7151:2024, so ask the supplier which standard applies.
