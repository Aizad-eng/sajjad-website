Equipment that helps prevent bed sores: mattresses, cushions and repositioning aids

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

Equipment that helps prevent bed sores (pressure ulcers) includes high-specification foam mattresses, air mattresses, pressure-redistributing seat cushions and repositioning aids. The UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) pairs this equipment with risk assessment and regular changes of position, so the care team should choose the item, not the shop.

Bed sores, also called pressure ulcers or pressure sores, are a real worry for families caring for someone who spends long hours in bed or in a chair. Equipment can help spread pressure, but it works alongside good care, not instead of it. This guide explains which equipment types the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the NHS mention, what the research says about air mattresses, and what to check before you buy. It does not cover wound treatment. For any skin change or wound, follow your doctor's or nurse's advice.

What are bed sores and who is most at risk?

Bed sores are areas of damaged skin and tissue caused by pressure, and they usually form over bony parts of the body. The NHS lists the heels, elbows, hips and tailbone as common places. It says the risk is higher for people who have difficulty moving, have had a pressure ulcer before, are underweight, have poor circulation or fragile skin, are seriously ill or have recently had surgery, or have problems feeling sensation or pain. NICE says all patients should be seen as potentially at risk.

Does equipment replace changing position?

No, equipment supports regular changes of position but does not replace them. NICE guideline CG179 says adults assessed as at risk should be encouraged to change position at least every 6 hours, and adults at high risk at least every 4 hours. If they cannot move themselves, carers should help, using suitable equipment if needed, and the agreed frequency should be written down. NICE also says not to use skin massage or rubbing to prevent pressure ulcers.

The NHS adds simple daily steps for people cared for at home: change position regularly, check the skin every day, and use specially designed mattresses and cushions. If you think someone is at risk, the NHS suggests asking a GP for a risk assessment so the care team can plan prevention.

Which mattress types are used to prevent bed sores?

NICE recommends a high-specification foam mattress as the starting point for adults at high risk, with dynamic (powered air) surfaces considered when foam is not enough. In CG179, NICE says to use a high-specification foam mattress for adults admitted to hospital and for adults assessed as high risk in community care. For adults who already have a pressure ulcer, it says to use high-specification foam, not standard foam, and to consider a dynamic support surface if foam does not spread the pressure enough.

What is the difference between reactive and alternating pressure air mattresses?

A reactive air mattress holds a steady pressure and moulds to the body, while an alternating pressure mattress uses a pump to move pressure around the body in cycles. Cochrane reviews published in 2021 describe reactive (static) surfaces as applying constant pressure unless the person moves or is repositioned, and active surfaces as regularly redistributing pressure by changing the points of contact.

The evidence is limited. The Cochrane review on reactive air surfaces for prevention found they may reduce new pressure ulcers compared with foam (risk ratio 0.42, 95% confidence interval 0.18 to 0.96; 4 studies, 229 people), rated as low-certainty evidence. Compared with alternating pressure air surfaces, the overall difference was uncertain, although one 14-day nursing home study favoured reactive air. A separate Cochrane review on surfaces for treating existing ulcers said the authors were uncertain about the relative effects of most surfaces. In short, no single mattress type is proven best for everyone, which is why the care team should choose. Our guide to air mattresses for bed sores covers the buying side.

What seat cushions help people who sit for long periods?

A pressure-redistributing cushion helps people who use a wheelchair or sit for long periods. NICE CG179 says to consider the seating needs of people at risk who sit for long periods, and to consider a high-specification foam or equivalent pressure-redistributing cushion for adults who use a wheelchair or sit for long periods. For children who use a wheelchair long term, NICE recommends regular wheelchair assessments with pressure relief. A cushion should match the seat size of the chair, so measure the wheelchair seat before ordering.

How is pressure on the heels reduced?

NICE asks the care team to agree a heel offloading plan for people at high risk of heel ulcers, rather than naming one product. CG179 says to discuss a strategy to offload heel pressure with the person and their family as part of their care plan. Because NICE does not specify the method, ask the nurse or doctor what they want used, and then source that item. For babies and children at risk of pressure ulcers on the back of the head, NICE mentions a suitable pillow or pressure-redistributing pad.

What other equipment supports prevention at home?

A hospital bed and repositioning aids make it easier for carers to change a person's position safely. NICE repeatedly refers to helping people reposition using appropriate equipment, and says healthcare staff should be trained in using repositioning equipment for children. A hospital bed with height and backrest adjustment can make turning and transfers easier for carers. NICE also mentions barrier preparations for people at high risk of moisture damage, such as those with incontinence. Skin products are outside the scope of this equipment guide, so ask the nurse which one to use.

What should you check before buying a pressure mattress or cushion?

Check that it fits the bed or chair, suits the user's weight, and will keep working in your home conditions. Useful questions to ask the supplier and the manufacturer's manual:

  • What are the mattress or cushion dimensions, and do they fit the existing bed frame or chair?
  • What user weight range does the manufacturer state?
  • Is it a full replacement mattress or an overlay that sits on top of the existing one?
  • For powered air mattresses: what does the pump do during a power cut, does it have an alarm, and how long does the mattress stay inflated without power?
  • Can the cover be wiped or washed, and is it waterproof?
  • Are spare covers, pumps or cells available?

Power cuts matter for powered mattresses. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) advises asking the manufacturer whether a device can run on batteries or a generator, and checking that settings have not changed when power returns.

Who should choose the equipment?

The care team should choose the equipment, as part of a written care plan. NICE says people at high risk should have an individualised care plan that takes into account their risk and skin assessment, the need for extra pressure relief at specific sites, their mobility and their equipment needs. Frequently asked questions

What is the best mattress to prevent bed sores?

NICE recommends a high-specification foam mattress for adults at high risk, and suggests considering a powered (dynamic) air surface if foam is not enough. Research has not shown one mattress type is best for everyone, so let the care team choose.

Is an air mattress better than foam for bed sores?

A 2021 Cochrane review found reactive air surfaces may reduce new pressure ulcers compared with foam, but rated the evidence as low certainty. The difference between air mattress types is uncertain.

Does an air mattress mean the patient no longer needs turning?

No. NICE still advises regular changes of position, at least every 6 hours for adults at risk and at least every 4 hours for adults at high risk, with help if they cannot move themselves.

Do wheelchair users need a special cushion?

NICE says to consider a high-specification foam or equivalent pressure-redistributing cushion for adults who use a wheelchair or sit for long periods. Match the cushion size to the chair seat.

What happens to an alternating pressure mattress during a power cut?

It depends on the model, so ask the manufacturer how long it stays inflated, whether it has an alarm, and whether it can run on a battery or generator.
