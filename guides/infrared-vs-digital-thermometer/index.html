Infrared vs digital thermometer: which is more accurate?

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

A digital probe thermometer used correctly is generally the more dependable choice. MedlinePlus says electronic probe thermometers are most often suggested and that ear models can be less accurate. Infrared forehead scanners can come close to probe accuracy, but readings depend on technique, distance and the room. Follow the manual and your doctor's advice.

Most homes and clinics in Pakistan choose between two kinds of thermometer: a digital stick thermometer that touches the body, and an infrared thermometer that reads heat from the ear or forehead. Infrared models are quicker and feel more modern, but speed is not the same as accuracy. This guide compares the two using guidance from MedlinePlus (the US National Library of Medicine), the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). It is about choosing equipment. For questions about a fever or illness, follow your doctor's advice.

What is the difference between an infrared and a digital thermometer?

A digital thermometer measures temperature through contact with the body, while an infrared thermometer measures the heat given off by the eardrum or forehead. MedlinePlus describes digital probe thermometers being used in the mouth, under the arm or in the rectum, and staying in place until the device beeps. The AAP describes ear (tympanic) thermometers as reading infrared heat from the eardrum, and forehead (temporal artery) thermometers as reading infrared heat from the forehead.

Forehead models include non-contact infrared thermometers (NCITs). The FDA notes that NCITs measure surface temperature and are held at a distance that is specific to each model, so the manual matters.

Which type is more accurate?

A digital probe thermometer used correctly is generally the more dependable choice, though a good forehead scanner can come close. MedlinePlus says electronic probe thermometers are most often suggested. It adds that some users find ear thermometers less accurate than probe thermometers, while electronic forehead thermometers are more accurate than ear models and similar in accuracy to probe thermometers. Plastic strip thermometers are the least accurate and are not recommended for general home use.

The AAP ranks measuring sites in a similar way. It says rectal temperatures are the most accurate, forehead is next, oral and ear readings are accurate if done properly, and armpit readings are the least accurate but can be used for screening at any age. In practice, the site you measure and how well you follow the instructions matter as much as the type of device.

Why do thermometers give different readings?

Readings differ partly because body temperature is not the same at every site. MedlinePlus gives typical differences compared with a mouth (oral) reading, while noting that the exact relationship between methods is unclear.

This is why a reading from an ear thermometer and one from an armpit thermometer taken a minute apart may not match, even if both devices are working. When you need to follow temperature over time, use the same thermometer at the same site each time.

The FDA notes that a typical normal temperature is 37.0 °C, but some studies show a normal range from 36.1 °C to 37.2 °C.

What affects the accuracy of an infrared thermometer?

Infrared readings are sensitive to the room, the skin and how the device is held. The FDA's advice for non-contact infrared thermometers says the manufacturer's instructions usually include these points:

  • Use the thermometer in a draft-free space, out of direct sun and away from heaters.
  • Room temperature is typically meant to be between 16 and 40 °C, with relative humidity below 85%.
  • Leave the thermometer in the room for 10 to 30 minutes before use so it can adjust.
  • The forehead should be clean, dry and uncovered. Head covers, heavy clothing and cosmetic wipes can change skin temperature.
  • Hold the sensor perpendicular to the forehead at the distance the manual gives, and keep the person still.
  • Keep the sensor clean and dry, and do not touch it.

For ear thermometers, the AAP advises waiting 15 minutes after coming in from the cold. MedlinePlus suggests waiting at least an hour after heavy exercise or a hot bath, and 20 to 30 minutes after smoking, eating or a hot or cold drink, before taking any temperature. Which thermometer is suggested for babies and young children?

Guidance differs between countries, so ask your child's doctor which method they want you to use. The AAP says rectal readings are the most accurate, especially for infants under 3 months, and that ear thermometers are not reliable for babies under 6 months because their ear canals are too narrow. It says oral readings are not recommended until age 4.

NICE guideline NG143 for children under 5 recommends an electronic thermometer in the armpit for infants under 4 weeks. For children aged 4 weeks to 5 years, it lists an electronic thermometer in the armpit, a chemical dot thermometer in the armpit, or an infrared ear thermometer. It advises against routinely using the oral and rectal routes in this age group, and says forehead chemical thermometers are unreliable. What standards do clinical thermometers follow?

The FDA's page on non-contact thermometers cites two standards: ASTM E1965-98, the specification for infrared thermometers used for intermittent patient temperature, and ISO 80601-2-56:2017, which covers the basic safety and essential performance of clinical thermometers. A thermometer made for body temperature should state the standard it meets in its manual. The FDA's advice applies to thermometers intended for a medical purpose, so choose one labelled for use on people. Our guide to thermometer accuracy standards explains these standards further.

Regulation also changes over time. At the time of writing (November 2026), the FDA has exempted certain clinical electronic thermometers from US premarket notification. Rules in Pakistan are set separately, so check the current rule with the Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan (DRAP) if you import devices.

Should you still use a mercury thermometer?

No, the AAP says not to use mercury thermometers. It explains that they can break and release toxic mercury, and suggests removing them from the home. MedlinePlus repeats the AAP's advice against glass mercury thermometers. A digital probe thermometer does the same job without the mercury.

Which one should a home or clinic buy?

For a dependable reading at home, a digital probe thermometer is the usual first choice, and an infrared model can be added for quick checks. For a clinic that sees many patients, the FDA notes that non-contact thermometers may reduce the risk of spreading infection, are easy to clean, and give rapid readings, as long as they are used as the manual directs.

  • Choose a thermometer labelled for medical use on people.
  • Check the stated accuracy and measuring range in the manual.
  • For ear models, check that probe covers are available to buy.
  • Check whether it shows Celsius, Fahrenheit or both, and pick the unit your doctor uses.

How do you clean each type?

Clean a digital probe thermometer before and after each use with cool soapy water or rubbing alcohol, as MedlinePlus advises. Infrared thermometers should be cleaned the way their manual describes. The FDA notes that most non-contact thermometers should never be put in water or other liquids.

Frequently asked questions

Is an infrared thermometer as accurate as a digital one?

Not always. MedlinePlus says electronic forehead thermometers can be similar to probe thermometers, but ear models may be less accurate, and all infrared readings depend on correct use.

Why does my forehead thermometer read lower than my digital one?

Temperature differs by body site. MedlinePlus says forehead scanner readings are most often 0.3 to 0.6 °C lower than oral readings.

Can I use an infrared thermometer outdoors?

The FDA advises using non-contact thermometers in a draft-free space out of direct sun, typically in room temperatures between 16 and 40 °C.

Are ear thermometers suitable for babies?

The AAP says ear thermometers are not reliable for babies under 6 months. Ask your child's doctor which method to use.
