Absorbable vs non-absorbable sutures: materials, sizes and how to order

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

Absorbable sutures, such as polyglactin 910 (Vicryl), poliglecaprone 25 (Monocryl), polydioxanone (PDS) and surgical gut, are broken down by the body over weeks to months. Non-absorbable sutures, such as nylon, polypropylene and silk, stay in the tissue unless removed. The surgeon chooses which to use, and buyers order by material, USP size and needle.

Sutures are ordered by material, size, length and needle, and a clinic may stock a dozen variations. The biggest split is between absorbable and non-absorbable thread. This guide explains that difference with product facts from manufacturers, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the ICRC (International Committee of the Red Cross) catalogue. It does not tell you which suture to use on a patient. That decision belongs to the surgeon.

What is the difference between absorbable and non-absorbable sutures?

Absorbable sutures lose their strength and are then broken down by the body over a set period, while non-absorbable sutures keep their material in the tissue unless they are removed. Both types are sterile threads intended for soft tissue approximation, which means holding tissue edges together.

For synthetic absorbable sutures, two numbers matter on the product sheet. The first is strength retention, the share of original strength left after a given number of weeks. The second is the absorption time, when the material has gone. Strength is lost well before the material disappears. The ICRC catalogue explains that polyglactin 910 is broken down by hydrolysis into glycolic and lactic acids, which the body then clears.

Which materials are absorbable sutures made from?

Common absorbable sutures are made from synthetic polymers such as polyglactin 910, poliglecaprone 25 and polydioxanone, or from natural surgical gut. Brand names you will see on quotes include Ethicon's VICRYL, MONOCRYL and PDS II, alongside generic sutures made from the same materials.

  • Polyglactin 910 (VICRYL): a copolymer of glycolide and lactide, supplied by ICRC as a coated, braided thread. The FDA groups glycolide and lactide sutures under one regulation, 21 CFR 878.4493, which allows monofilament or multifilament, coated or uncoated, dyed or undyed versions.
  • Rapid polyglactin (Vicryl Rapid): a faster-absorbing version that ICRC lists for short-term support of skin and mucosa.
  • Poliglecaprone 25 (MONOCRYL): a monofilament, sold dyed or undyed.
  • Polydioxanone (PDS II): a monofilament with a long absorption time.
  • Surgical gut, plain or chromic: made from the connective tissue of beef or sheep intestine, as defined by the FDA in 21 CFR 878.4830.

How long do absorbable sutures keep their strength?

It depends on the material, from about one to two weeks for rapid and poliglecaprone types to six weeks or more for polydioxanone. Which materials are non-absorbable sutures made from?

The main non-absorbable sutures are nylon, polypropylene and silk, and the FDA defines each one in its own regulation. All three are class II devices in the US, subject to special controls.

  • Nylon (polyamide), for example ETHILON: made from nylon 6 and nylon 6,6 (21 CFR 878.5020). It may be monofilament or multifilament.
  • Polypropylene, for example PROLENE: a monofilament made from a polyolefin polymer (21 CFR 878.5010). The US device database lists PROLENE as a blue polypropylene monofilament.
  • Silk, for example PERMA-HAND or MERSILK: a multifilament thread made from fibroin, a protein produced by the silkworm (21 CFR 878.5030).

Nylon and polypropylene sutures must also meet the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) monograph for non-absorbable surgical sutures.

What is the difference between monofilament and braided sutures?

A monofilament suture is a single strand, while a braided (multifilament) suture is made of many fine strands woven together. Both absorbable and non-absorbable sutures come in each form.

Braided threads are often coated. ICRC says the coating on its polyglactin suture reduces capillarity, the wicking of fluid along the braid, and helps the thread pass smoothly through tissue. MONOCRYL, PDS II and PROLENE are monofilaments, while silk is always multifilament under its FDA definition. Write monofilament or braided on your order if the surgeon has a preference.

How do USP suture sizes work?

USP suture sizes describe thread diameter, and more zeros mean a finer thread, so 4-0 is thinner than 2-0. The scale runs from 11-0, the finest, up to size 7, the thickest, and many packs also show a metric (EP) gauge in tenths of a millimetre.

What needle details do you need when ordering sutures?

You need the needle curve, point type and length, because the same thread is sold with many different needles. In ranges such as the ICRC polyglactin sutures, the thread is fixed into an eyeless (swaged) needle. Thread sold without a needle is called a ligature.

  • Curve: usually a fraction of a circle, such as 3/8 or 1/2.
  • Point: round body (taper point) for tissue that tears easily, or cutting points. Ethicon also lists spatula points for fine work.
  • Needle length: in mm. ICRC lists 18 to 48 mm for its polyglactin range.
  • Thread length: in cm, for example 45, 70, 75 or 90 cm.
  • Manufacturer code: the most reliable way to order the same product again, for example an Ethicon product code.

Who chooses between absorbable and non-absorbable sutures?

The surgeon or clinician doing the procedure chooses the suture material, size and needle for each case. A buyer's job is to stock what the clinical team lists and to match it exactly.

Labelled uses also differ between products. ICRC, for example, lists standard polyglactin for general soft tissue approximation and ligation but not for vascular or neurological tissue, and the rapid version not for ligation. Ask the clinical team for a written list with brand or material, size, needle and length.

How should sutures be stored and checked on delivery?

Store sutures in their sealed packs, away from heat and direct sunlight, and never re-sterilise them. The ICRC catalogue gives a maximum storage temperature of 25 degrees C and says any unused sutures from an opened pack must be thrown away.

On delivery, check the sterile packaging is sealed, the expiry date, the lot number and that the material, size and needle code match your order. Ethicon lists ethylene oxide as the sterilisation method for VICRYL, and a broken seal means the contents can no longer be treated as sterile.

Frequently asked questions

How long does Vicryl take to dissolve?

Ethicon lists VICRYL (polyglactin 910) as absorbed in 56 to 70 days, with 75% of its strength left at 2 weeks and 25% at 4 weeks. The rapid version is absorbed in about 35 to 42 days according to the ICRC catalogue.

Is nylon suture absorbable?

No. The FDA classifies nylon (polyamide) suture as a non-absorbable surgical suture, along with polypropylene and silk.

Which suture is thicker, 2-0 or 4-0?

2-0 is thicker. In the USP system more zeros mean a finer thread, so in the ICRC table 2-0 measures 0.25 to 0.29 mm and 4-0 measures 0.15 to 0.19 mm.

Is PDS absorbable or non-absorbable?

PDS II (polydioxanone) is absorbable. Ethicon lists it as a monofilament absorbed in 182 to 238 days, with 60% of its strength left at 6 weeks.

Can opened sutures be re-sterilised?

No. The ICRC catalogue says sutures must not be re-sterilised and any unused sutures from an opened pack should be discarded, because re-sterilising weakens the thread.
