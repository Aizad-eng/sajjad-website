How to choose an oxygen concentrator: flow, purity, alarms and power

Updated November 1, 2026 · 9 min read

Quick answer

To choose an oxygen concentrator, start with the doctor's prescription, then pick a machine whose flow range covers the prescribed litres per minute. Check oxygen purity at that flow, the alarms, power rating, noise and filter needs, and decide between a home continuous-flow unit and a portable pulse-dose unit. Oxygen should only be used on prescription.

An oxygen concentrator is one of the most important pieces of equipment a family can buy, and one of the easiest to get wrong. Two machines that look the same can differ in flow range, purity, alarms and power needs. This guide explains what to compare, using published specifications from real models as examples. It does not tell you how much oxygen anyone needs. Only a doctor can decide that, so follow your doctor's advice.

Do you need a prescription before choosing an oxygen concentrator?

Yes, the prescription comes first because it sets the flow rate and how many hours a day oxygen is used. The NHS says home oxygen should only be used if a GP or specialist prescribes it, and warns that buying oxygen online and using it without a prescription could damage the brain, heart and lungs or even cause death. In the UK, patients are assessed at a specialist clinic, which may include blood oxygen checks and walking tests.

Manufacturers say the same. The Drive DECO2 5 litre manual states that the user should use the device according to the manufacturer's instructions and the doctor's advice, and is not allowed to adjust the device flow. Its US labelling also restricts sale to the order of a physician.

How does an oxygen concentrator work?

A concentrator takes in room air, removes most of the nitrogen and delivers the remaining oxygen-rich air through tubing. The DECO2 manual describes the common pressure swing adsorption (PSA) design: a compressor pushes filtered room air into one of two sieve beds filled with a material that holds back nitrogen under pressure. Oxygen passes through to a storage tank and then out through an adjustable flowmeter. Meanwhile the second bed releases its nitrogen, and the two beds keep swapping roles.

The NHS describes a home concentrator as about the size of a bedside table, plugged into an ordinary electrical socket. Because it makes oxygen from air, it does not need refilling like a cylinder, but it does need power all the time it is in use.

Which flow range do you need: 5 litre or 10 litre?

Choose a machine whose flow range comfortably covers the flow written on the prescription. Home concentrators are usually sold as 5 litre or 10 litre models, which refers to the maximum flow in litres per minute (L/min). Real examples at the time of writing (November 2026):

What oxygen purity should the specification show?

Look for the oxygen concentration stated at your prescribed flow, after warm-up, and for a built-in indicator that warns when purity falls. The DECO2 manual gives 90% plus or minus 3% at 0.5 L/min and 93% plus or minus 3% from 1 to 5 L/min, measured after a 30 minute warm-up. The DeVilbiss 10L lists 87% to 96% across 2 to 10 L/min and has an oxygen sensing device fitted as standard.

The DECO2 shows purity with coloured lights: green at 82% or above (normal), yellow between 73% and 82% (keep using and call the provider), and red below 73% (switch to backup oxygen). Ask any supplier for the same kind of detail for the model they offer.

Which alarms and safety features matter?

At minimum, look for a power failure alarm, a low oxygen concentration alarm and a flow or pressure alarm. The DECO2 manual lists alarms for power loss, high and low pressure, low oxygen concentration and low flow, plus automatic shutdown for current overload and compressor overheating, and an hour meter that records total running time. An alarm only helps if someone can hear it, so think about where the machine and the carer will be at night.

Home concentrator or portable concentrator: which one?

A home (stationary) concentrator suits people who mainly use oxygen at home, while a portable concentrator suits people who need oxygen while going out. CAIRE, a concentrator maker, describes stationary units as delivering continuous flow across a full range of flow rates, with up to 50 feet of tubing for moving around the home. Portable units are light enough to carry and often deliver oxygen in pulses: a burst (bolus) with each breath, sensed through the nasal cannula, instead of a steady stream.

The Inogen Rove 6, for example, is a pulse dose system with six settings and a battery life of up to 6.25 hours with the single battery or 12.75 hours with the double battery, according to Inogen. Pulse dose and continuous flow are not interchangeable, so only choose a portable unit if the prescriber confirms it suits the patient. See portable oxygen concentrators for more.

How do power supply and power cuts affect the choice?

Check that the voltage and frequency on the rating label match the mains supply where the machine will be used, and plan for power cuts before the machine arrives. The two Drive home models above are US listings rated at 120 V and 60 Hz, so ask the supplier for the version made for your local supply. The DECO2 manual says not to use extension cords, notes that the unit has no internal battery and is not intended for emergency use, and recommends running it for at least 30 minutes at a time.

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) advises people who rely on powered devices to ask the manufacturer whether the device can run on batteries or a generator, never to plug in a wet cord or device, and to check that settings have not changed when power returns. Ask the prescriber what backup oxygen they want you to keep for power cuts.

Where should the concentrator be placed and what safety rules apply?

Place it in a well-ventilated room with space around it, away from heat, flames, smoking and oily products. The DECO2 manual asks for at least 10 cm (4 inches) of clear space on all sides, away from walls, curtains, heaters and deep carpets, and says no smoking in the same room and no open flames within 2 metres. The NHS advice is stricter on flames: keep the equipment at least 3 metres from open-flame appliances such as a gas cooker, and at least 1.5 metres from other electrical appliances and heat sources. The NHS also warns against oil-based creams such as Vaseline and against flammable liquids or aerosols near the equipment.

Room conditions matter too. The DECO2 manual gives an operating range of 5 to 40 °C and 15% to 80% relative humidity, and says performance stays consistent up to 1,828 metres above sea level but drops at higher altitude. Hot summers, dust and high hill areas are worth discussing with the supplier.

What maintenance should you plan for?

Plan for regular filter cleaning, filter replacement and humidifier care, plus access to spare parts. The DECO2 manual says to clean the cabinet filter every 7 days with mild soap and warm water, to replace (not clean) the intake filter, to change the humidifier water every day and to wash the bottle weekly. It warns that dusty or smoky rooms need more frequent filter changes, and notes that the nasal cannula and humidifier bottle are not included. The DeVilbiss 10L listing shows separate cabinet, intake, compressor and final bacteria filters, and a limited 3 year warranty in the US. Ask who will service the machine locally and whether filters are available. If you only need oxygen for a short time, read oxygen concentrator: buy or rent.

Frequently asked questions

Can I buy an oxygen concentrator without a prescription?

You should not use one without a prescription. The NHS warns that using oxygen without a prescription can damage the brain, heart and lungs or even cause death, and the prescription sets the flow you need.

Is a 5 litre or 10 litre oxygen concentrator better?

Neither is better in general. Pick the one whose flow range covers the prescribed flow; for example the DeVilbiss 10L starts at 2 L/min, while the DECO2 5L covers 0.5 to 5 L/min.

What oxygen purity should a concentrator give?

Check the purity stated at your flow after warm-up. The DECO2 lists 93% plus or minus 3% from 1 to 5 L/min, and its indicator warns when purity falls below 82%.

Can a home oxygen concentrator run during load-shedding?

Most home units need mains power and have no internal battery; the DECO2 manual says so directly. Ask the manufacturer about battery or generator use and ask the prescriber what backup oxygen to keep.

What is the difference between pulse dose and continuous flow?

Continuous flow gives a steady stream at a set litres per minute, while pulse dose releases a burst of oxygen with each breath. Only use a pulse dose unit if the prescriber confirms it suits the patient.
