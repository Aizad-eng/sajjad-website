Urine bag types: drainage, leg, night and paediatric bags explained

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

The main urine bag types are leg bags (often 350 ml to 750 ml) strapped to the leg by day, larger 2 litre night or bedside drainage bags, urine meter bags with an hourly measuring chamber, and smaller paediatric bags. Check capacity, tube length, outlet tap, non-return valve and whether the bag is sterile.

A urine bag looks like a simple item, but the wrong capacity, tube length or outlet can make daily care harder for patients and staff. This guide explains the main types of urine collection bag, the fittings you will see on product sheets, the standard that applies and what to write on an order. It covers equipment only. Which bag a patient uses is decided by their doctor or nurse.

What are the main types of urine bag?

The main types are leg bags worn on the body during the day, larger night or bedside drainage bags that hang beside the bed, urine meter bags with a measuring chamber, and smaller bags made for children. All of them collect urine from a catheter or other collection device into a closed bag.

Within these groups you will also see sterile and non-sterile bags, drainable bags with a tap, and single-use bags that are thrown away instead of emptied. The table below shows typical examples from an NHS formulary and the ICRC (International Committee of the Red Cross) supply catalogue.

What is a leg bag and what sizes does it come in?

A leg bag is a small drainage bag strapped to the leg under clothing, with a tap at the bottom for emptying. The NHS says it can be worn on whichever leg is more comfortable.

At the time of writing (November 2026), the NHS Lanarkshire primary care formulary lists sterile leg bags in 350 ml, 500 ml and 750 ml sizes, with a short or long inlet tube and either a T tap or a lever tap. Each pack of 10 includes an overnight connector and elastic straps. Washable leg bag sleeves are sold by leg measurement, for example small for 24 to 39 cm and medium for 36 to 55 cm.

Tube length is chosen to suit where the bag sits on the leg, so ask the user or nurse before ordering. NHS advice is to empty a leg bag when it is around half to three-quarters full, and to change leg bags and catheter valves every 7 days. The NHS Lanarkshire list gives 5 to 7 days for its leg bags. Always follow the label and your nurse's instructions.

What is a night bag and how is it different from a drainage bag?

A night bag is a larger bag, usually 2 litres, used overnight so the user does not need to empty a leg bag while asleep. The NHS explains that it connects to the leg bag or to a catheter valve and sits on a stand beside the bed, near the floor.

The NHS notes that single-use night bags are usually used to reduce the risk of infection. NHS Lanarkshire lists a non-sterile 2000 ml single-use night bag with a 100 cm tube, a non-return valve and a twist-off outlet for people who use leg bags. It separately lists a sterile, drainable 2000 ml night bag with a 120 cm tube, a tap, a non-return valve and an antibacterial filter, for patients such as those who are bed bound and do not wear a leg bag. The same formulary says drainable night bags are not recommended for connection to leg bag systems, for infection control reasons.

In hospitals, a similar 2 litre bag is often called a drainage or bedside bag. The ICRC catalogue describes one with markings every 100 ml, a non-kinking inlet tube of about 90 cm, a non-return valve, a drain valve and two eyelets for hanging.

Should urine bags be sterile or non-sterile?

Bags connected directly to an indwelling catheter are normally supplied sterile, while some non-sterile bags are sold for other uses. The ICRC catalogue lists a sterile 2 litre bag for indwelling urinary catheters and a non-sterile version for incontinence sheaths (condom catheters) and gastric tubes.

The same catalogue notes a trend since 2011 for bulk-packed bags to be sold clean but not sterile. The NHS Lanarkshire single-use night bag for leg bag users is also non-sterile. If you need sterile bags, say so clearly on the order and check that each pack is marked sterile.

What do the valves, taps and tubes on a urine bag do?

Each fitting on a urine bag has one job, and product sheets list them by name, so it helps to know what each part does.

  • Non-return (anti-reflux) valve: a one-way valve at the inlet that stops urine flowing back towards the catheter. ICRC places it inside the bag at the urine entry point.
  • Outlet tap: lets the bag be emptied without disconnecting it. Product lists show T taps, lever taps and twist-off outlets.
  • Inlet tube: carries urine from the catheter. Look for non-kinking tubing and check the length in cm.
  • Connector: the end that fits the catheter. ICRC describes a multi-sized connector with a protective cap.
  • Sampling port: a needle-free port on the tubing so a sample can be taken without opening the system.
  • Graduations: volume markings, for example every 100 ml.
  • Material: ICRC lists PVC, polypropylene or EVA for the bag and medical-grade PVC for the tubing.

Which standard applies to urine collection bags?

The main international standard is ISO 8669-2, published in Europe as EN ISO 8669-2, which sets performance requirements and test methods for urine collection bags. It covers body-worn and non body-worn bags, both open-ended and closed.

According to the Irish standards shop listing, it does not apply to urine bags for paediatric use, and it replaced earlier parts 2, 3 and 4 of ISO 8669. The ICRC catalogue requires its 2 litre bags to comply with EN ISO 8669-2. Ask the supplier which standard a product is made to and request the manufacturer's data sheet. What are paediatric urine bags and urine meter bags?

Paediatric urine bags are smaller-volume bags made for children, and because ISO 8669-2 excludes paediatric bags, each product's own specification matters more. Ask the maker for capacity, how the bag attaches, whether it is sterile and the age or size range stated on the label.

Urine meter (urometer) bags have a small graduated chamber at the top, so staff can read hourly output without emptying the main bag. The chamber then drains into the larger bag below.

How should urine bags be handled with a catheter?

Keep the bag below the level of the bladder, off the floor, and keep the drainage system closed. These points come from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guideline on catheter-associated urinary tract infection and from NHS advice.

  • Keep the catheter and tubing free from kinks so urine can flow.
  • Empty the bag regularly into a separate clean container for each patient, and do not let the outlet touch the container.
  • Take small samples from the needle-free sampling port after cleaning it, rather than opening the system.
  • Use a stand or hanger so a night bag sits beside the bed, not on the floor.

These are equipment handling points. For anything about the catheter or the patient's condition, follow your doctor's or nurse's advice.

What should a clinic write on a urine bag order?

Write the bag type, capacity, tube length, outlet, valves, sterility and pack size, so the supplier can quote the exact product.

  1. Bag type: leg bag, single-use night bag, drainable bedside bag, paediatric bag or urine meter bag.
  2. Capacity in ml.
  3. Tube length: short or long, or the length in cm.
  4. Outlet: T tap, lever tap or twist-off.
  5. Non-return valve, sampling port and filter, if needed.
  6. Sterile or non-sterile.
  7. Accessories: straps, sleeves by leg size, hangers or stands, catheter valves.
  8. Quantity, pack size and any brand preference.

Frequently asked questions

What size is a urine night bag?

Most night bags on product lists hold 2000 ml (2 litres). NHS Lanarkshire lists both a single-use 2000 ml night bag with a 100 cm tube and a sterile drainable 2000 ml bag with a 120 cm tube.

How often should a leg bag be changed?

NHS guidance says leg bags and catheter valves should be changed every 7 days, and one NHS formulary gives 5 to 7 days. Follow the product label and your nurse's advice.

Can a night bag be connected to a leg bag?

Yes. The NHS says a night bag connects to the leg bag or a catheter valve, and one NHS formulary uses single-use non-sterile night bags for this while advising against connecting drainable night bags to leg bag systems.

What is an anti-reflux valve on a urine bag?

It is a one-way (non-return) valve at the inlet of the bag that stops urine flowing back towards the catheter. Most leg, night and drainage bags on formulary and catalogue lists include one.

Does ISO 8669-2 cover paediatric urine bags?

No. The EN ISO 8669-2 listing states that it does not apply to urine bags for paediatric use, so check the paediatric product's own specification and label.
