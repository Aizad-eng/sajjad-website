Minor surgery instrument set: contents, uses and what to check before you order

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

A minor surgery instrument set is a small kit of hand instruments for short procedures such as wound suturing. A typical set holds a needle holder, fine iris or stitch scissors, toothed and non-toothed Adson tissue forceps and a curved mosquito artery forceps. Sets come as reusable stainless steel instruments or as sterile single-use packs.

Clinics, GP practices, emergency rooms and small hospitals often need a compact set of instruments for minor procedures. This guide lists what a typical minor surgery set contains, what each piece is for, the sizes found in commercial packs and what to write when you order. It is a buying guide for equipment, not a guide to performing procedures. For clinical technique, follow your training and your facility's protocols.

What is a minor surgery instrument set?

A minor surgery instrument set is a small group of hand instruments packed together for short procedures close to the skin surface. The World Health Organization (WHO) manual Surgical Care at the District Hospital lists wound suturing, debridement and dressing of wounds, and incision and drainage among the procedures expected even at a small hospital or health centre with a sparsely equipped operating room.

The same WHO manual sorts surgical instruments into four broad groups: forceps and other instruments for holding tissue, needle holders, scissors and retractors. A minor set usually takes one or two items from the first three groups and leaves out large retractors.

You will see these sets sold under several names, such as minor op pack, minor operations pack, minor surgeries pack or suture set. Contents vary by maker, so read the item list rather than relying on the name.

What instruments are in a minor surgery set?

A typical minor surgery set contains one needle holder, one pair of fine scissors, a toothed and a non-toothed tissue forceps, and one small artery forceps. The table compares two sterile single-use packs sold in the UK, using the contents each supplier lists.

What does each instrument in the set do?

Each instrument in a minor set has its own main job.

  • Needle holder: grips the curved suture needle. Most patterns have a ratchet that locks the jaws, so the user does not have to keep squeezing. The Kilner pattern appears in both packs above. Our needle holder types guide compares Mayo-Hegar, Halsey, Olsen-Hegar and others.
  • Iris stitch scissors: small scissors with fine tips. Iris scissors were first designed for eye surgery, and minor packs list a straight 11.5 cm iris stitch version. See our surgical scissors types guide for Mayo, Metzenbaum and others.
  • Adson toothed forceps: non-locking tissue forceps with a 1 x 2 tooth pattern at the tip, used for holding tissue.
  • Adson non-toothed forceps: smooth or cross-hatched tips for handling swabs, dressings and sutures.
  • Mosquito artery forceps: a locking forceps (haemostat) that clamps a bleeding vessel. The Halsted mosquito is one of several named haemostat patterns covered in our surgical forceps types guide.
  • Scalpel handle: WHO notes that most procedures use a No. 3 handle with a No. 10, 11 or 15 blade, while the larger No. 4 handle takes No. 20 or 22 blades.

A University of Bristol clinical skills sheet makes a useful point for buyers: artery forceps should not be used as needle holders. If your team keeps borrowing the mosquito forceps to drive needles, you need a second needle holder, not a stronger forceps.

Should you buy a reusable set or single-use packs?

Choose single-use packs when you cannot reliably clean and sterilise instruments between patients, and reusable stainless steel sets when you have a working sterilisation routine and steady demand.

  • Single use: DD Group describes the Instrapac pack as sterile and single use, with instruments made of medical grade surgical stainless steel and the contents wrapped in a sterile field. Each pack is opened once and thrown away, so the cost per procedure is the pack price.
  • Reusable: WHO advises sterilising all surgical instruments and supplies after use and storing them protected and ready for the next use. That needs cleaning materials, a steriliser such as an autoclave, packaging and staff time. Our sterilisation basics guide explains the steps.

You can also keep both: reusable sets for planned lists and a small stock of sterile packs for outreach camps or days when the steriliser is busy. Work out your expected number of procedures per month before deciding.

What sizes should minor surgery instruments be?

In the two packs above, instruments run from 11.5 cm (scissors) to 14 cm (needle holder). WHO advises choosing the shortest instrument that will comfortably reach the operative site.

Longer patterns exist for deeper work. Mayo scissors, for example, are typically 150 to 170 mm long. Needle holders also come in different lengths and jaw sizes for different suture sizes. One supplier, World Precision Instruments, matches a 14 cm Halsey needle holder to 3-0 to 6-0 sutures and a 15 cm Mayo-Hegar to 1-0 to 4-0 sutures. If your team uses a wide range of sutures, one needle holder may not cover them all.

Which standards apply to minor surgery instruments?

Three International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards are the most relevant to a minor set. Check the current edition when you order, as the editions below are those listed at the time of writing (November 2026).

  • ISO 7151:2024: general requirements and test methods for non-cutting articulated (jointed) surgical instruments. The 2024 edition follows the 1988 edition.
  • ISO 7741:1986: scissors and shears used in surgery, covering materials, heat treatment and hardness, corrosion resistance, workmanship and cutting ability.
  • ISO 7153-1:2016: the metals commonly used to make standard surgical instruments for general surgery, orthopaedics and dentistry.

Ask the supplier which standards the instruments are made to and which steel is used.

How can you tell if a set is in good working order?

A set is in good working order when forceps tips meet cleanly, scissors cut easily and the needle holder grips a needle firmly. WHO warns that forceps that cross at the tip, scissors that do not cut easily and needle drivers that do not grip the needle securely can be frustrating and dangerous.

When a new set arrives, a simple hands-on check helps:

  1. Count every item against the packing list. WHO describes counting instruments before a case, before closure and at the end as standard practice, so a complete list from the start makes this easier.
  2. Close each forceps and look at the tips. They should meet without crossing or gapping.
  3. Close each ratchet and open it again. It should hold at every step and release smoothly.
  4. Open and close the scissors along the full length of the blade. The movement should be even, without grinding.
  5. Look for rust spots, pitting, cracks or rough edges, and report them to the supplier straight away.

What should you include in a quote request for a minor set?

Include the type of set, every instrument by pattern name and length, and the quantity. A clear list avoids substitutions.

  • Reusable or single use, and sterile or non-sterile.
  • Each instrument by pattern and length, for example Kilner needle holder 14 cm, iris scissors straight 11.5 cm, Adson forceps toothed 12.5 cm.
  • Straight or curved, toothed or non-toothed, and whether you want tungsten carbide inserts on the needle holder or scissors.
  • Whether you need a tray, case or sterilisation container with the set.
  • The number of sets, and any standard or steel grade you require.

Frequently asked questions

What is included in a minor surgery instrument set?

A typical set includes a needle holder, fine scissors such as iris stitch scissors, toothed and non-toothed Adson forceps and a small curved artery forceps. Single-use packs often add swabs, drapes and a tray with pots.

Does a minor surgery set include a scalpel?

Many packs do not. The two UK packs compared in this guide list no scalpel handle or blade, so check the item list and order a handle and blades separately if needed.

Are single-use minor surgery packs sterile?

Packs such as the Instrapac minor operations pack are described by the supplier as sterile and single use. Always confirm the sterile marking and expiry date on each pack's label.

Can artery forceps be used as a needle holder?

A University of Bristol clinical skills sheet states that artery forceps should not be used as needle holders. Keep a proper needle holder in every set.

Which ISO standards apply to minor surgery instruments?

ISO 7151 covers non-cutting articulated instruments, ISO 7741 covers surgical scissors and ISO 7153-1 covers the metals used. Confirm the current edition with the supplier when you order.
