Stainless steel grades for surgical instruments: what ISO 7153-1 says

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

Most reusable surgical instruments are made from martensitic stainless steel, a hardenable chromium and carbon steel that holds an edge and springs back. Austenitic stainless steel, such as 304 and 316 types, resists corrosion better but is not suited to cutting instruments. ISO 7153-1:2016 from the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) lists suitable metals by instrument type.

Almost every reusable surgical instrument is sold as "surgical steel", but that phrase says very little. This guide explains what the international standard ISO 7153-1 covers, how martensitic and austenitic stainless steels differ, which steel suits which instrument, and what to ask a supplier before you order.

What is ISO 7153-1 and what does it cover?

ISO 7153-1 is the international standard that lists the metals commonly used to make standard surgical instruments. The current text is the third edition, ISO 7153-1:2016, dated 1 October 2016. It replaced the 1991 edition, which covered stainless steel only, and widened the scope to metals in general.

The standard applies to instruments used in general surgery, orthopaedics and dentistry. It says it is not intended for special applications such as implantology and minimally invasive surgery, although parts of it may still apply. Europe adopted the same text as EN ISO 7153-1:2016.

The standard also makes a point buyers should remember. Choosing the steel is still the instrument maker's decision, because design and production methods matter too. So the standard tells you which materials experience has shown to be suitable, not which brand is good.

Is there an official "surgical steel" grade?

No, there is no formal definition of "surgical stainless steel". Makers and sellers use the phrase for any corrosion-resistant steel. Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) notes in its instrument catalogue that the most common steels sold under this name are austenitic 316 and martensitic 420 and 440 types.

Stainless steel itself is defined by chemistry: at least 10.5% chromium and no more than 1.2% carbon by mass. Chromium gives corrosion resistance. Carbon allows the steel to be hardened by heat treatment, but more carbon also makes it less ductile.

What is the difference between martensitic and austenitic stainless steel?

Martensitic steel is hard and springy, while austenitic steel resists corrosion better but is too soft for cutting edges. A 2014 paper by the Surgical Materials Testing Laboratory (SMTL) in Wales describes martensitic steel as strong and hard, containing carbon and chromium, with moderate corrosion resistance. It notes that austenitic alloys contain chromium and nickel, resist corrosion better (especially from chloride), but are unsuitable for high-strength and cutting uses.

  • Martensitic (420 and 440 families): can be hardened by heat treatment, holds an edge, may be slightly magnetic.
  • Austenitic (304 and 316 families): chromium and nickel steels; 316 also contains molybdenum and is often called marine grade. Good corrosion resistance and strength, but not hardenable for blades.

A simple magnet test therefore tells you little about quality. A slightly magnetic scissor blade can be exactly the right steel.

Which steel is used for which instrument?

Instruments that cut or clamp are made from martensitic steel, and simple static items can be austenitic. The MSF catalogue groups instruments by how they work:

MSF also stresses finishing. The steel must be carefully polished, and the quality of the polish affects corrosion resistance. Heat treatment matters too: poorly hardened forceps can bend on first use.

What do grade names like 420, 1.4021 and X20Cr13 mean?

They are different naming systems for similar steels. ISO 7153-1:2016 lists stainless grades by the European material number and short name from EN 10088-1 (for example 1.4021, X20Cr13). The older 1991 edition used reference letters, and many suppliers still quote those letters or American-style numbers such as 420.

What are tungsten carbide inserts and when are they worth it?

Tungsten carbide inserts are hard-metal jaws or blades fitted to a steel instrument to make it last longer. MSF lists tungsten carbide with a cobalt binder for forceps and needle holders, tungsten carbide with a nickel binder for forceps, scissors and needle holders, and cobalt-chrome-tungsten alloy for scissors.

Tungsten carbide is roughly twice as stiff as steel and very wear resistant. For high-use needle holders and scissors this can mean a longer working life. Ask the supplier which insert material is used and whether worn inserts can be replaced.

Are titanium instruments better than stainless steel?

Titanium instruments are lighter and non-magnetic, but they are a specialist choice rather than a general upgrade. MSF notes that pure titanium (ISO 5832-2, grades 1 to 4) is used for retractors and probes, and titanium alloy (ISO 5832-3) for forceps, scissors, needle holders and other parts. Titanium instruments are often anodised blue, which also cuts glare. For a routine clinic set, good martensitic stainless steel remains the normal choice.

Why do stainless steel instruments still rust or stain?

Stainless steel stays stainless only while its thin chromium oxide surface layer is intact. This passive layer forms when chromium reacts with oxygen. A UK instrument maker, Surgical Holdings, says it is only 1 to 3 nanometres thick at first, forms naturally over about 48 hours, and can be damaged by handling, chemicals, repair work and sharpening. Re-passivation after repair is recommended where possible.

The SMTL paper names chloride as the main chemical threat. Chloride can damage the passive layer, from small black spots to deep pits. It cites guidance that chloride below 120 mg per litre carries a low risk at room temperature, with the risk rising with heat, poor drying and residues left on the instrument. Silicates, iron, copper and manganese in water may discolour steel, but the same paper reports this as harmless staining rather than corrosion. Good cleaning and drying protect the steel; see our sterilisation basics guide.

What should you ask a supplier about steel grade?

Ask for the exact material, not just "German steel" or "surgical grade". A useful request covers:

  • The steel grade by EN number (for example 1.4021) or by the ISO 7153-1 material, for each instrument type.
  • Whether cutting instruments are hardened, and to what hardness. ISO 7153-1 refers to the Vickers (ISO 6507-1) and Rockwell (ISO 6508-1) hardness test methods.
  • Which product standards the maker follows, such as ISO 7741 for scissors, ISO 7151 for non-cutting hinged instruments, and ISO 13402 for resistance to autoclaving and corrosion.
  • Whether jaws or blades have tungsten carbide inserts.
  • The maker's cleaning and sterilisation instructions.

Pattern numbers help you compare like with like. MSF notes that many instrument labels carry a Medicon reference in the form 11-22-33, which defines the article but can be met by other makers of the same quality. Frequently asked questions

Is 420 stainless steel good for surgical instruments?

Yes, 420-type martensitic steel is widely used for surgical and dental instruments because it can be hardened and holds an edge. Its corrosion resistance is lower than 316, so good cleaning and drying matter.

Is 316 stainless steel used for surgical instruments?

316 is an austenitic steel with good corrosion resistance, but it cannot be hardened for cutting edges. It suits items such as boxes and some static instruments rather than scissors or forceps.

Why is my stainless steel forceps slightly magnetic?

Martensitic stainless steels, the type used for most forceps and scissors, may be slightly magnetic. Magnetism alone does not show poor quality.

What does ISO 7153-1 specify?

ISO 7153-1:2016 lists the metals commonly used to make standard surgical instruments for general surgery, orthopaedics and dentistry, and which materials suit which instrument types. It does not replace the maker's own material choice.

Why do new stainless steel instruments develop rust spots?

Spots usually mean the protective chromium oxide layer has been damaged, often by chloride, residues or poor drying. Instruments that have been sharpened or repaired may also need re-passivation.
