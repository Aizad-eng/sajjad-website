Oxygen concentrator: buy or rent in Pakistan?

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

Whether to buy or rent an oxygen concentrator in Pakistan depends on how long the doctor expects oxygen to be needed, who will maintain the machine and how you will cover power cuts. Renting can suit short or uncertain needs. Buying can suit long-term use, since a concentrator can run for years with care. Use oxygen only as prescribed.

Families often need an oxygen concentrator at home at short notice, after a hospital stay or a new diagnosis, and the first question is whether to buy one or rent one. There is no single right answer. This guide sets out the factors on both sides and the checks that apply to any machine. It is not medical advice: the need for oxygen, the flow rate and the hours of use are for your doctor to decide.

Do you need a prescription for an oxygen concentrator?

You should only use home oxygen if a doctor has prescribed it. The UK National Health Service (NHS) says home oxygen is for people whose GP or specialist has prescribed it, after an assessment that may include a blood test, a pulse oximeter reading and a walking test. The prescription sets the flow rate and how many hours a day oxygen is needed, and those two numbers decide which machine is suitable. The NHS describes a concentrator as the usual choice when oxygen is needed for most of the day, including during sleep.

How does an oxygen concentrator work?

A concentrator takes in room air, removes most of the nitrogen and delivers concentrated oxygen through a flowmeter. The World Health Organization (WHO) explains that air passes through filters and a compressor, then into sieve beds of zeolite, a mineral that holds nitrogen under pressure. This process, called pressure swing adsorption, produces up to 95.5% oxygen. Stationary units can deliver continuous flow up to 10 litres per minute (LPM). Portable units usually give 3 LPM or less, and many deliver oxygen only when the user breathes in (pulse flow). See 5 litre vs 10 litre concentrators for capacity.

When does renting an oxygen concentrator make sense?

Renting usually makes sense when the need is short or uncertain. Consider renting if:

  • the doctor expects oxygen to be needed only for a recovery period
  • you want the provider to handle cleaning, servicing and replacement if the machine fails
  • you need a machine today and want time to decide on a model

Before you sign, ask the rental provider in writing about the deposit, the minimum rental period, who pays for repairs, how fast a faulty machine is replaced, how the machine is cleaned between users and how many hours it has already run.

When does buying an oxygen concentrator make sense?

Buying usually makes sense when the need is long term or open ended. The WHO says concentrators are designed to run 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, for up to 5 years or more, given a reliable power supply and regular maintenance. When you own the machine you know its full history and hours, and you choose the model. The WHO also notes that spare parts are not interchangeable between models, so pick a model whose filters and parts can be obtained in Pakistan. What should you check on any oxygen concentrator, bought or rented?

Check the same safety and performance points whether you buy or rent. The WHO technical specifications, based on the ISO 80601-2-69 standard, call for:

  • oxygen concentration above 82%, with an oxygen monitor and alarm if it falls below 82%
  • alarms for no flow, high or low pressure, power failure and high temperature
  • a time meter that records total hours of use
  • a flowmeter marked in steps of 0.5 LPM or finer
  • cleanable filters, with cleaning steps in the manual
  • no more than 50 dB(A) of noise
  • a power rating and plug that match your mains supply
  • a user manual and a service manual with a spare parts list

The WHO adds that heat and high humidity can lower oxygen output, which matters in rooms without air conditioning. For model-level advice, see how to choose an oxygen concentrator.

How much power does a concentrator use, and what about load shedding?

Stationary concentrators draw roughly 280 to 600 watts depending on the model, according to the WHO. As simple arithmetic, a 400 W machine running for 24 hours uses 9.6 kWh in a day, so check your own tariff. Because a concentrator stops when the power stops, the WHO says a back-up oxygen cylinder is essential during power failures or repairs. It also recommends at least a voltage stabiliser accepting ±20% of the rated input and a surge protector, and suggests a UPS or battery system where cuts are short. These points apply whether you buy or rent.

Is a used or refurbished oxygen concentrator a middle option?

A used machine can cost less than a new one, but you take on the same risks as renting without a provider to fix problems. If you consider one, check it as carefully as a rental. Ask for the reading on the time meter, which the WHO says every concentrator should have, so you know how many hours it has run. Switch it on and confirm the oxygen monitor does not raise a low concentration alarm, which the WHO links to readings below 82%. Ask whether the sieve beds have been replaced, since the WHO notes that drawing more than the rated flow can wear them early and that replacement needs trained technical staff. Ask for the user manual and confirm filters for that model are available. Our guide to refurbished vs new medical equipment covers the wider trade-offs.

How do you keep a home oxygen concentrator safe?

Keep it away from flames, heat and smoking, and keep the room ventilated. The NHS advises keeping the equipment at least 3 metres from open flames such as gas cookers, and at least 1.5 metres from heat sources and other electrical appliances. MedlinePlus, from the US National Library of Medicine, gives about 2 metres (6 feet) from heat and flame sources. Where advice differs, follow your machine's manual and keep the larger distance. Do not allow smoking near oxygen, avoid aerosols and flammable products, and ask your care team before using oil-based creams such as petroleum jelly. Clean the filters as the manual describes; the WHO suggests keeping a spare filter so the machine can run during cleaning.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to buy or rent an oxygen concentrator?

It depends on how long you need it. Renting usually suits short or uncertain needs, and buying usually suits long-term use, so compare total rent over the expected period with the purchase price.

Can I use an oxygen concentrator without a prescription?

No. Use home oxygen only if a doctor has prescribed it, and follow the prescribed flow rate and hours.

How long does an oxygen concentrator last?

The WHO says concentrators are designed to run around the clock for up to 5 years or more, given reliable power and regular maintenance.

What happens to an oxygen concentrator during load shedding?

It stops when the power stops. The WHO says a back-up oxygen cylinder is essential, and recommends a voltage stabiliser and surge protector, with a UPS or battery system for short cuts.

What should I check on a rented oxygen concentrator?

Check the hour meter, the oxygen purity alarm, the filters and the manual, and confirm in writing who handles repairs and how fast a faulty machine is replaced.
