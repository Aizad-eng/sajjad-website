How to use a glucometer correctly: step by step

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

To use a glucometer correctly, wash and dry your hands, insert a new test strip made for your meter, prick a fingertip with a fresh lancet, apply a small drop of blood, and read the result after a few seconds. Record it, dispose of the lancet safely, never share the meter, and follow your doctor's advice on when to test.

A glucometer (blood glucose meter) gives a reading in a few seconds, but only when the strip, the blood drop and the technique are right. This guide covers the device steps: preparing the meter, getting a good sample, storing strips, checking the meter with control solution and avoiding common errors. It does not explain what your numbers mean or what to do about them. Follow your doctor's advice on when to test and how to act on results.

What do you need before you use a glucometer?

You need the meter, test strips made for that exact meter, a lancing device with new lancets, and the user manual. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) says you should read and understand your meter's instructions before you test, because models differ in how the strip goes in, where the blood goes and what each error code means.

  • The meter, switched on and with a working battery
  • Test strips listed in the meter's manual, within their expiry date
  • A lancing device and a new lancet
  • Control solution for that meter, if the maker supplies one
  • A notebook or the meter's app for records
  • A closed, hard container for used lancets

How do you use a glucometer step by step?

The routine is: check the meter, clean your hands, prepare the strip and lancet, prick a fingertip, apply blood, read, record and dispose.

  1. Check the meter. Confirm it has power. Most meters run a short electronic self-check when switched on. If an error code appears, look it up in the manual before testing.
  2. Wash and dry your hands. Use soap and warm water, then dry completely. If your hands are cold and blood is hard to get, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) suggests warming them before you wash.
  3. Load a new lancet. Set the depth on the lancing device as the manual describes.
  4. Take out one strip and close the vial straight away. Check the expiry date on the vial or box first.
  5. Insert the strip and apply blood in the order your manual gives. Many current meters ask you to insert the strip first and then touch the drop to the strip edge. Some instructions describe placing the drop on the strip before inserting it.
  6. Get a good drop. Massage or shake your hand, prick a fingertip, and if needed squeeze gently from the base of the finger, as the CDC describes.
  7. Read the result. It appears after a few seconds.
  8. Record it. Note the time and anything that might have affected the reading.
  9. Dispose and wash again. Throw away the strip and lancet, then wash your hands. The FDA advises washing again to lower the risk of spreading blood-borne diseases.

Why does washing your hands matter for a glucometer reading?

Clean, dry hands are the first step in both the FDA and CDC instructions, and the FDA lists hand hygiene among the factors that affect meter accuracy. Anything left on the fingertip can end up in the blood drop. Wash with soap and warm water and dry fully before you prick. Avoid testing straight after handling food, cream or anything sticky without washing first.

How should glucometer test strips be stored?

Keep strips in their original container with the lid tightly closed, away from moisture, humidity and extreme heat or cold. The CDC notes that these conditions can damage strips, and the FDA says to store the meter and strips as the manufacturer directs. In much of Pakistan, summer heat and monsoon humidity make this rule matter more, so do not leave strips in a parked car, near a stove or in a damp bathroom.

  • Use a new strip for every test. The FDA says to always use new test strips.
  • Keep strips in the vial they came in, unless the manual says otherwise.
  • Check the expiry date. If the leaflet gives a use-within period after opening, write the opening date on the vial.
  • Buy a pack size you can use before it expires. Our glucometer strips guide covers compatibility and expiry.

What is glucometer control solution and when should you use it?

Control solution is a test liquid that checks whether your meter and strips are working together correctly. You test it like a blood drop, and the result should fall inside the range printed on the test strip vial label. The FDA suggests a control test:

  • when you open a new container of strips
  • every so often during normal use
  • after the meter has been dropped
  • when results seem unusual

If the control result is outside the printed range, do not rely on the meter until the problem is found. Repeat the test, check the strip expiry date, and contact the manufacturer's support line listed in the manual.

Can you test somewhere other than a fingertip?

Some meters allow testing at sites other than the fingertip, but the FDA warns that these results can be inaccurate when glucose is changing quickly. Only use an alternate site if your meter's manual allows it. The FDA says to use the fingertip instead if you:

  • are ill, stressed, have just eaten or have just exercised
  • have just taken insulin
  • think your glucose may be low, or do not usually notice symptoms of low glucose
  • get results that do not match how you feel

Most wrong readings come from strips, technique or conditions the meter was not designed for. The table below lists the factors named by the FDA, the CDC and one manufacturer notice.

How should you record and share glucometer readings?

Most meters store results in memory, and some send them to a phone app. For example, Roche lists the Accu-Chek Guide meter with 720 diary records and Bluetooth transfer to the mySugr app at the time of writing (November 2026). If you do not use an app, keep a written log with the date, time and any notes. The FDA suggests taking your meter to your healthcare provider, who can watch your technique and compare your meter with a laboratory test. The CDC says your doctor will tell you when and how often to check.

Can a glucometer be shared between family members?

No. The FDA says over-the-counter glucose meters are for single-patient use because sharing carries a risk of passing on blood-borne infections. The CDC adds that monitoring equipment, especially lancets, should never be shared, even within a family. Each person who tests should have their own meter and lancing device. If you run a clinic that tests many patients, tell the supplier so the right type of meter is quoted.

Frequently asked questions

Do I put blood on the strip before or after inserting it into the glucometer?

Follow your meter's manual. Many current meters ask you to insert the strip first and then touch the blood drop to the strip edge, while some instructions describe the reverse.

Can I reuse a glucometer test strip?

No. The FDA says to always use a new test strip for each test.

How often should I use control solution?

The FDA suggests a control test when you open a new strip container, every so often during use, after dropping the meter and when results seem unusual. The result should fall within the range printed on the strip vial.

Why is my glucometer giving different readings each time?

Common causes are unwashed hands, damaged or expired strips, too little blood and extreme heat or humidity. If readings still seem wrong, run a control test and ask your doctor to compare your meter with a lab test.

Can two people use the same glucometer?

No. The FDA says over-the-counter meters are for single-patient use, and the CDC says lancets and monitoring equipment should never be shared, even with family.
