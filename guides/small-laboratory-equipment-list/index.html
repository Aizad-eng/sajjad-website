Small clinical laboratory equipment list: what a basic lab needs

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

A small clinical laboratory equipment list starts from the test menu. Common items are a centrifuge, a haematology analyser, a chemistry analyser or point-of-care meters, a microscope, micropipettes, a reagent fridge and test strips, plus safety items: a handwashing sink, eyewash station, lab coats, eye protection, sharps containers and a way to decontaminate waste.

A small clinical laboratory, whether attached to a clinic or standing alone, is built around its test menu. Two labs of the same size can need very different equipment because one runs blood counts and chemistry while the other mostly does rapid tests and urine strips. This guide shows how to build the list, using published guidance from the World Health Organization (WHO), the US CDC and NIH biosafety manual, and the Punjab Healthcare Commission. Requirements vary by province and by the tests you run.

How do you decide what equipment a small lab needs?

You decide by writing the test menu first, because each test sets its own equipment, consumables and safety needs. For primary care, the WHO PEN implementation tools (2013) list blood glucose test strips, urine protein strips and urine ketone strips as core items, and add blood cholesterol, lipid profile, serum creatinine, troponin test strips and urine microalbuminuria strips when resources permit. That gives a realistic starting menu for a clinic lab. Write down each test, the expected daily volume, and whether it will run on a strip, a point-of-care meter or a bench analyser.

Should a small lab buy a point-of-care meter or a bench analyser?

A point-of-care meter suits low volumes and single tests, while a bench analyser suits higher volumes and wider panels. As an example of a point-of-care device, the HemoCue Hb 301 measures haemoglobin in capillary, venous or arterial whole blood using about 10 µL per test, with a stated range of 0.9 to 25.6 g/dL, and is aimed at anaemia screening in primary care and blood donation. A bench haematology analyser gives a full blood count but needs reagents, controls and a service plan. Compare running cost per test, not just purchase cost, and confirm how reagents will be supplied.

What safety equipment does a clinical laboratory need?

A clinical lab needs basic biosafety equipment as well as its analysers. The CDC and NIH manual Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories (BMBL, 6th edition) lists these points for Biosafety Level 2 work, the level suitable for agents linked to human disease that pose moderate hazards:

  • A handwashing sink, located near the exit door
  • An eyewash station readily available in the laboratory
  • Lab coats, gowns or uniforms worn only in the lab
  • Eye and face protection for work that may cause splashes or sprays
  • Puncture-resistant sharps containers placed as close to the point of use as possible
  • A method to decontaminate all laboratory waste, such as an autoclave, chemical disinfection, incineration or another validated method
  • Benchtops that resist water, heat, solvents, acids, alkalis and other chemicals, and chairs with a non-porous, easy-clean cover

BMBL is a US manual, so use it as a reference alongside the rules that apply to your lab.

Does a small lab need a biological safety cabinet?

Whether you need a biological safety cabinet should come from a risk assessment of the work you do. WHO's Laboratory biosafety manual (4th edition, 2020) takes a risk-based approach, matching safety measures to the actual risk of each activity. BMBL says that at Biosafety Level 2, properly maintained biological safety cabinets or other containment devices should be used when possible for procedures that can create infectious aerosols or splashes, such as pipetting, centrifuging, mixing and opening containers of infectious material. If a lab cannot do a procedure in a cabinet, BMBL calls for protective equipment and procedures based on a risk assessment. It also mentions sealed rotors or centrifuge safety cups for some work, which is worth checking when you choose a centrifuge.

What should go in a lab spill and cleaning kit?

A lab spill kit should let trained staff contain, disinfect and clean up a spill without touching broken glass or sharps by hand. BMBL says a spill procedure should be written and posted in the lab, that work surfaces should be decontaminated after work and after any spill or splash, and that broken glassware should be picked up with a brush and dustpan, tongs or forceps rather than by hand. It also says lab equipment should be decontaminated routinely, after spills or splashes, and before repair, maintenance or removal from the lab. For your order, that means a disinfectant suitable for your work, absorbent material, a dustpan and brush or tongs kept only for the lab, spare gloves and eye protection, biohazard bags and a printed spill procedure. If your lab uses vacuum lines, BMBL says they should be protected with liquid disinfectant traps and in-line HEPA filters or their equivalent.

Which Pakistani standards apply to clinical laboratories?

In Punjab, the Punjab Healthcare Commission publishes Minimum Service Delivery Standards (MSDS) for clinical laboratories, mainly in the private sector. At the time of writing (November 2026), its download page describes this set as 37 standards with 118 indicators. It also lists separate guidelines for collection centres, based on the clinical laboratory standards. The documents are requested through a form on the Commission's website. Outside Punjab, ask your provincial health department or healthcare regulator which standards apply.

How should a lab handle its waste?

A lab should separate its waste at the point where it is produced and treat infectious waste safely. WHO says about 85% of health-care waste is general and non-hazardous, while about 15% is hazardous and may be infectious, chemical or radioactive. WHO encourages separating waste properly and, where feasible, treating it by methods such as autoclaving, microwaving or chemical treatment rather than incineration. Plan sharps boxes, colour-coded bins, leak-proof containers for transport and an arrangement with an approved waste handler.

How should you plan a laboratory equipment order?

Plan the order around your test menu, daily volume and how each analyser will be supported after installation. Before you ask for quotes:

  1. List every test with its expected daily number.
  2. Choose strip, meter or analyser for each test.
  3. For each analyser, ask which reagents and controls it uses and whether they can be supplied locally on a regular basis.
  4. Ask about installation, user training, spare parts and the warranty.
  5. List consumables separately: tubes, tips, slides, stains, strips and sharps boxes.
  6. Check your power supply and plan a UPS or stabiliser if the analyser maker recommends one.

Frequently asked questions

What is the first step in equipping a small lab?

Write the test menu with expected daily volumes. Each test decides whether you need strips, a point-of-care meter or a bench analyser, and what consumables follow.

Does every clinical lab need an autoclave?

BMBL says a Biosafety Level 2 lab should have a method to decontaminate all laboratory waste, such as an autoclave, chemical disinfection, incineration or another validated method. An autoclave is one option, not the only one.

Do I need a biological safety cabinet for a small lab?

It depends on a risk assessment of your work. BMBL advises using cabinets when possible for procedures that can create infectious aerosols or splashes at Biosafety Level 2.

Which standards apply to labs in Punjab?

The Punjab Healthcare Commission publishes Minimum Service Delivery Standards for clinical laboratories, described as 37 standards and 118 indicators at the time of writing, plus guidelines for collection centres.

What should I ask before buying a lab analyser?

Ask which reagents and controls it needs, whether they can be supplied regularly, and what installation, training, spare parts and warranty are included.
