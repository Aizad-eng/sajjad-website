Surgical and isolation gown levels explained: AAMI PB70 Level 1 to Level 4

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

AAMI PB70 rates gowns from Level 1, the lowest liquid barrier, to Level 4, the highest. Levels 1 to 3 are tested with water spray and water pressure, and only Level 4 is tested against a virus surrogate under ASTM F1671. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recognises ANSI/AAMI PB70 for gown barrier claims.

Gown boxes often say "Level 2" or "Level 3" with little else, and suppliers use many names: surgical gown, isolation gown, procedure gown, theatre gown. This guide explains the AAMI PB70 levels behind those labels, the difference between surgical and isolation gowns, what a critical zone is, and what to write on your order so you get the barrier level you expect. It covers equipment only. Which gown is worn for which task is decided by your facility's infection control policy.

What is AAMI PB70?

ANSI/AAMI PB70 is a US standard that rates protective apparel and drapes for health care by how well they block liquids, on a scale from Level 1 to Level 4.

Its full title is "Liquid barrier performance and classification of protective apparel and drapes intended for use in health care facilities". The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recognises PB70 as the classification system for gown barrier claims, and the standard sets both the test methods and the labelling. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) PPE information page refers to both the 2012 and 2022 editions, so ask which edition a gown was tested to at the time of writing (November 2026).

What do AAMI PB70 Levels 1, 2, 3 and 4 mean?

Level 1 is the lowest liquid barrier and Level 4 is the highest, and each level has its own pass mark in set laboratory tests.

PB70 levelTest and pass mark (CDC summary)Example uses (FDA)
Level 1 (minimal risk)Water spray impact (AATCC 42): 4.5 g or lessBasic care, standard isolation, visitor cover gowns
Level 2 (low risk)AATCC 42: 1.0 g or less, and water pressure (AATCC 127): 20 cm or moreBlood draws, suturing, ICU, pathology lab
Level 3 (moderate risk)AATCC 42: 1.0 g or less, and AATCC 127: 50 cm or moreArterial blood draw, IV line insertion, emergency room, trauma
Level 4 (high risk)Viral penetration (ASTM F1671, Phi-X174 virus surrogate): no penetration at 2 psi (13.8 kPa)Long, fluid-heavy procedures, surgery, when pathogen resistance is needed

For drapes, Level 4 uses the synthetic blood test ASTM F1670 instead of F1671.

Is a Level 3 gown protection against blood and viruses?

No, only a Level 4 gown is tested against a virus surrogate, so Levels 1 to 3 cannot be treated as virus-proof.

The CDC page on PB70 explains that Levels 1 to 3 are tested with water only. Blood has a lower surface tension than water, so water test results cannot be used to predict how a fabric behaves against blood in the ASTM F1671 virus test. The CDC also warns that fluid-resistant garments of any level do not always protect the wearer from blood and other infectious material. If a procedure calls for blood and virus resistance, write "AAMI PB70 Level 4" on the order and ask for the F1671 report.

What is the difference between a surgical gown and an isolation gown?

A surgical gown is sterile and worn in surgery, while an isolation gown protects staff and their clothing during patient care, and the FDA notes that non-sterile gowns are used in non-surgical situations.

The FDA groups gowns as follows:

  • Surgical gown: worn in surgery to protect patient and staff. Class II, needs FDA clearance. Must be sterile.
  • Surgical isolation gown: for medium to high contamination risk, with larger critical zones than a surgical gown. Class II.
  • Non-surgical isolation gown: for minimal or low risk isolation, not for surgery or invasive procedures. Class I, exempt from premarket review.
  • Non-surgical, non-isolation gowns: Class II if they claim a moderate or high barrier, Class I if minimal or low.

The CDC isolation guideline says an isolation gown should cover the arms and the front of the body from the neck to the mid-thigh or below, and that facilities should stock several sizes. It also says isolation gowns are removed before leaving the patient care area, with the contaminated outside turned inward and rolled into a bundle for disposal. That means one gown per room entry where gowns are in use, which matters when you work out order quantities.

What is a critical zone on a gown?

A critical zone is the part of the gown most likely to meet fluid, and it must reach the gown's rated barrier level.

For surgical gowns, the FDA says the critical zone covers at least the front from shoulders to knees and the arms from the wrist cuff to above the elbow. The back may be non-protective. For surgical and non-surgical isolation gowns, the whole gown except bindings, cuffs and hems is a critical zone, and the seams must perform like the rest of the gown. So a "Level 3 surgical gown" and a "Level 3 isolation gown" do not cover the same area. Ask the supplier for a diagram of the protected zones.

Which other gown standards might you see on a label?

You may also see ASTM F2407, ASTM F3352, EN 13795 or Chinese standards, depending on where the gown is made and sold.

The World Health Organization (WHO) technical specification of 7 August 2020 lists these for buyers:

  • Isolation gowns: AAMI PB70 Levels 1 to 3 with ASTM F3352, or Level 4 with ASTM F3352, or EN 13034 Type PB, or ISO 16604 Class 5.
  • Surgical gowns: AAMI PB70 with ASTM F2407, or EN 13795 in Europe, or YY/T 0506 in China, plus EN 556 if sterile.

EN 13795 uses "standard performance" and "high performance" instead of levels. The WHO table gives its water pressure test as at least 20 cm of water for standard performance and at least 100 cm for high performance in critical areas.

Should you buy sterile or non-sterile, disposable or reusable gowns?

Buy sterile gowns for surgery and non-sterile gowns for isolation and general care, and choose disposable or reusable based on your laundry setup.

The FDA requires surgical gowns to be sterile, with a sterility assurance level of 10 to the power of minus 6, and asks makers to state the sterilisation method. Cloth gowns not used in a sterile field may be reused after laundering with an enzymatic detergent or under the hospital's own procedures. The WHO specification adds that reusable gowns should still meet their minimum performance after the maximum number of wash cycles the maker suggests. The FDA also advises checking the shelf life, and says expired gowns should only be used for training. See sterilisation basics for clinics for more.

What should you write on a gown order?

State the gown type, the PB70 level, sterility and sizes, and ask for the test reports.

  • Type: surgical, surgical isolation or isolation gown.
  • Barrier: AAMI PB70 level (or EN 13795 performance), and the edition.
  • Sterile or non-sterile, and the sterilisation method if sterile.
  • Material: disposable nonwoven or reusable woven. WHO lists both.
  • Sizes: WHO lists S, M, L and XL, with mid-calf length.
  • Cuff type, closure (ties or other) and pack count.
  • Test reports for each level claimed, plus shelf life.

Gowns are worn with gloves, so you may also need surgical gloves, along with masks or N95 respirators and caps and shoe covers. More items are under surgical disposables.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Level 2 isolation gown?

It is a gown that passes the AAMI PB70 Level 2 tests: water spray impact of 1.0 g or less and water pressure resistance of at least 20 cm. The FDA gives blood draws, suturing and ICU work as example low-risk uses.

Which gown level is impermeable to viruses?

Only Level 4 is tested for viral penetration, using ASTM F1671. Levels 1 to 3 are tested with water only and cannot be treated as virus-proof.

Are surgical gowns always sterile?

Yes. The FDA requires surgical gowns to be supplied sterile, with a sterility assurance level of 10 to the power of minus 6.

What is the difference between a surgical gown and an isolation gown?

A surgical gown is sterile and protects mainly the front and sleeves in surgery. An isolation gown has critical zones over the whole gown except bindings, cuffs and hems.

Can expired gowns be used?

The FDA says expired gowns should only be used for training and demonstration, where barrier protection is not needed. Check the shelf life on the label.
