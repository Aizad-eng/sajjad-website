IV giving sets and infusion sets explained: drop factor, filters and types

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

An IV giving set (infusion set) is the sterile, single-use tubing that carries fluid from a bag or bottle to the IV cannula. Standard adult sets usually deliver 20 drops per ml, paediatric burette sets 60 drops per ml, and blood transfusion sets use a 170 to 200 micron filter instead of the fine filter in a standard set.

IV giving sets are among the most used disposables in any ward or clinic, and the packs can look almost identical. The differences that matter are the drop factor, the filter, the length, the connectors and whether the set is meant for fluids, blood or a burette. This guide explains each part and what to put on an order. It covers equipment only. Fluid choice, volume and rate are set by the doctor.

What is an IV giving set?

An IV giving set, also called an infusion set or administration set, is a sterile, single-use length of tubing that carries fluid from a bag or bottle to the patient's IV cannula. Gravity feed sets run without a pump, and the flow is adjusted with a clamp on the tube.

The international standard for these products is ISO 8536-4:2019, Infusion equipment for medical use, Part 4: Infusion sets for single use, gravity feed. It sets requirements for the sets and names their components. At the time of writing (November 2026), ISO lists the 2019 edition as current with a revision in progress.

What are the parts of an IV giving set?

A standard giving set has a spike, an air vent, a drip chamber with a filter, tubing, a roller clamp, an injection site and a patient connector. Product sheets from the UNFPA (United Nations Population Fund) and B. Braun describe the same basic layout.

  • Spike (piercing pin): pushes into the fluid bag or bottle and comes with a protective cap.
  • Air inlet: a vent next to the spike, fitted with a bacteria-retaining filter on the UNFPA and B. Braun sets.
  • Drip chamber: a clear chamber where drops can be seen and counted. It usually contains a fluid filter of about 15 micrometres to catch particles.
  • Tubing: often about 150 cm long, although some sets are much longer. One B. Braun set measures 264 cm.
  • Roller clamp: squeezes the tube to set the flow rate.
  • Injection site: a Y-shaped port, often needle-free, for adding drugs into the line.
  • Patient connector: a Luer or Luer lock end that fits the IV cannula.

What does drop factor mean on an infusion set?

Drop factor is the number of drops that make up 1 ml of fluid in that set's drip chamber, and it is printed on the packaging. Sets are grouped as macrodrip, usually 10 to 20 drops per ml, and microdrip, 60 drops per ml.

Standard adult sets are usually 20 drops per ml. B. Braun's Intrafix Primeline, for example, is specified at 20 drops equal to 1 ml plus or minus 0.1 ml of distilled water, and the B. Braun CARESAFE set is listed at 20 drops per ml. Paediatric burette sets usually deliver 60 drops per ml.

How do you work out drops per minute from the drop factor?

Drops per minute equals the volume in ml multiplied by the drop factor, divided by the time in minutes. This is simple arithmetic on the label value. The volume and time always come from the prescription.

For example, 1000 ml over 8 hours (480 minutes) through a 20 drops per ml set works out to 1000 x 20 / 480, which is about 42 drops per minute. With a 60 drops per ml microdrip set, drops per minute equal ml per hour, because the 60 cancels out. Using the wrong drop factor in this sum gives the wrong rate, which is why the pack value matters. Follow your hospital's protocol and the doctor's instructions.

What is a paediatric burette set?

A paediatric burette set is a giving set with a graduated chamber, called a burette, between the bag and the drip chamber, so a measured volume can be run in. The ICRC (International Committee of the Red Cross) catalogue describes a 150 ml burette marked every 1 ml, with a 60 drops per ml drip chamber.

That ICRC set also has an injection port on top of the burette, a valve that stops flow once the set volume has run through, a 15 to 20 micrometre filter in the drip chamber, and about 150 cm of tubing. The catalogue labels it not for blood transfusion and not for use for more than 24 hours.

What is the difference between an IV giving set and a blood transfusion set?

A blood transfusion set has a coarse 170 to 200 micron filter that removes clots while letting red cells and platelets through, while a standard IV set has a fine filter of about 15 microns. Australian Red Cross Lifeblood warns that using an IV set for blood may cause under-infusion or damage to red cells (haemolysis).

Lifeblood lists red cells, platelets, fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate, whole blood and granulocytes among the products that need a blood set, and suggests storing blood sets apart from IV sets to avoid mix-ups. The UNFPA infusion set listing also states it is not for blood, blood products or light-sensitive products. Keep the two types clearly labelled in your store.

Which features should you compare between giving sets?

Compare the drop factor, filter, length, connectors, injection sites, materials and the maximum use time printed by the maker. Two sets with the same drop factor can still differ a lot.

  • Connector: Luer slip or Luer lock. The UNFPA set uses a Luer lock connector.
  • Materials: look for DEHP-free or PVC-free tubing and latex-free parts if your facility requires them. B. Braun's Intrafix Primeline is listed as DEHP-free and latex-free, and the CARESAFE set as not made with PVC or DEHP.
  • Injection sites: one or more Y-sites. The CARESAFE set has two needle-free sites and a back-check valve.
  • Priming volume: the fluid needed to fill the line, 24.0 ml on the CARESAFE set.
  • Use time: the UNFPA set lists a maximum of 72 hours, and the ICRC paediatric set 24 hours. Follow the label and your facility's policy.
  • Pump use: if you use an infusion pump, ask for the set that the pump maker lists for that model.
  • Sterilisation: the UNFPA set lists ethylene oxide or gamma irradiation.

What should you write on an IV giving set order?

State the set type, drop factor, length, filter, connector and materials, plus the quantity, so the supplier quotes like for like.

  1. Type: standard giving set, paediatric burette set, blood transfusion set or pump set.
  2. Drop factor: 20 or 60 drops per ml, or as specified.
  3. Tubing length in cm.
  4. Filter: about 15 micron for fluids, 170 to 200 micron for blood.
  5. Connector: Luer slip or Luer lock.
  6. Injection sites and whether they are needle-free.
  7. DEHP-free, PVC-free or latex-free if required.
  8. Box quantity and brand preference.

Frequently asked questions

What is the drop factor of a standard IV giving set?

Most standard adult giving sets deliver 20 drops per ml, and the value is printed on the pack. Macrodrip sets in general range from 10 to 20 drops per ml.

What is a 60 drops per ml set used for?

A 60 drops per ml set is a microdrip set, often built into paediatric burette sets, for small or precise infusions. With this drop factor, drops per minute equal ml per hour.

Can a normal IV set be used for blood?

Blood services such as Australian Red Cross Lifeblood say no, because the fine 15 micron filter can slow the flow or damage red cells. Blood needs a transfusion set with a 170 to 200 micron filter.

What filter is in an IV giving set?

Standard giving sets usually have a fluid filter of about 15 micrometres in the drip chamber to trap particles, plus a bacteria-retaining filter on the air vent. Blood sets use a coarser 170 to 200 micron filter.

Which standard covers IV infusion sets?

ISO 8536-4:2019 covers single-use, gravity feed infusion sets. At the time of writing a new edition is being developed, so check which edition the supplier's data sheet cites.
