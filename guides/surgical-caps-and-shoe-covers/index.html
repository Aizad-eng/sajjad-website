Surgical caps and shoe covers explained: types, materials and how to order

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

Surgical caps cover the hair and shoe covers go over footwear to help keep germs, fluids and dirt out of operating theatres. Shoe covers are typically light disposable items, and caps come in disposable and cloth versions. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lists surgical caps and operating-room shoe covers as Class I surgical apparel under 21 CFR 878.4040.

Surgical caps and shoe covers are cheap, high-volume items, so they are easy to order in a hurry and get wrong. A theatre that wanted full hair cover may receive thin caps that slip, and a ward may receive plastic covers when staff wanted fabric ones. This guide explains what these items are for, the main types, how they are classed by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), when a workplace rule calls for them and what to write on an order. It is a buying guide. Your facility's infection control policy decides who wears what, and when.

What are surgical caps and shoe covers used for?

They are surgical apparel worn to help stop germs, body fluids and particles passing between staff, patients and the clean area.

US rule 21 CFR 878.4040 defines surgical apparel as devices worn by operating room staff during surgery to protect both the patient and the staff from the transfer of microorganisms, body fluids and particulate material. Its list of examples names surgical caps, hoods, masks and gowns, operating room shoes and shoe covers, and isolation masks and gowns. Scrub suits and dresses are left out of that rule.

Caps keep hair and the scalp covered. Shoe covers go over normal footwear so that dirt from outside is not carried into clean areas, and so that shoes are not soiled.

What types of surgical caps are there?

The common types are bouffant caps, fitted surgeon caps, hoods and full head covers.

  • Bouffant cap: a loose, full cap meant to hold all the hair inside. Wikipedia's entry on scrubs describes it as probably the most widely used hospital cap.
  • Surgeon cap or skull cap: a closer-fitting cap. In 2016 there was a debate in the US over cloth or disposable surgical caps versus mandatory bouffant caps, so some hospitals now specify one style only.
  • Hood: covers more of the head than a cap. The FDA lists "Hood, Surgical" as its own product code.
  • Full-face cover: Wikipedia notes full-face hats were designed for men with beards.
  • Disposable head scarf or hijab: some hospitals in the UK and US provide disposable hijabs for staff who keep their head covered in theatre.

What types of shoe covers are there?

Shoe covers range from low covers that go over the shoe only to taller boot covers that reach up the leg.

Wikipedia notes that shoe covers are usually made of light disposable materials such as polypropylene, polyethylene or other nonwoven fabric, chosen for durability, moisture resistance and easy disposal. Within that, buyers usually choose between:

  • Nonwoven fabric covers: fabric-like covers, for example nonwoven polypropylene.
  • Plastic film covers: for example polyethylene.
  • Boot covers: taller covers that reach up the leg. US rule 29 CFR 1910.1030 mentions boots alongside shoe covers.
  • Conductive shoe covers: the FDA lists "Shoe and Shoe Cover, Conductive" as a separate product code. If your theatre specifies them, order them by that name and ask for the test data.

Some covers also have a textured or anti-slip sole. If grip matters on your floors, ask for it on the order.

How are surgical caps and shoe covers classed by the FDA?

In the US they are Class I devices that are exempt from FDA premarket notification, which is the lowest risk group.

Under 21 CFR 878.4040, surgical gowns and surgical masks are Class II, while other surgical apparel is Class I and exempt from premarket notification. The FDA product classification database lists these codes at the time of writing (November 2026):

When does a workplace rule require caps and shoe covers?

The US bloodborne pathogens rule requires them when heavy contamination can reasonably be expected.

29 CFR 1910.1030 says surgical caps or hoods and shoe covers or boots must be worn where gross contamination can reasonably be anticipated, and gives autopsies and orthopaedic surgery as examples. The same rule says protective equipment is only "appropriate" if it does not let blood or other infectious material pass through to the worker's clothes, skin or mucous membranes. It also makes the employer responsible for providing, cleaning, replacing and disposing of that equipment at no cost to staff. This is a US rule, but many hospitals use similar wording in their own policies.

Are caps and shoe covers sterile?

Whether a cap or cover is sterile depends on the product, so check the label and say what you need.

If your theatre needs sterile items for a particular procedure, write "sterile" on the order and ask for the sterilisation method on the label. A sterile pack and a bulk non-sterile carton are different products with different prices, so a quote is only comparable when both sides mean the same thing.

How many caps and shoe covers does a clinic need?

Work it out from how many people enter the clean area each day and how often they change.

A simple method is to count staff and visitors per shift, multiply by the number of times each person changes, and add a margin for torn or dropped items. For shoe covers, confirm whether a pack size is counted in pieces or in pairs before you compare quotes.

For example, if six people work in a theatre and each changes cap and shoe covers three times a shift, that is 18 caps and 18 pairs of shoe covers per shift. Over 26 working days that becomes 468 caps and 468 pairs, before any margin. Run the same sum for each area and add the totals before you ask for a quote, and ask the supplier to state the price per carton and the pieces in each carton.

What should you write on a cap or shoe cover order?

Name the type, material, size and pack count, plus any special feature.

  • Cap type: bouffant, tie-back surgeon cap, hood or beard cover.
  • Cap size, so the cap covers all the hair.
  • Shoe cover type: low cover or boot cover, and the height for boot covers.
  • Material: nonwoven polypropylene or polyethylene film, and the fabric weight if the supplier states it.
  • Sole: plain or anti-slip.
  • Colour, if your facility uses colours to tell areas or staff groups apart.
  • Sterile or non-sterile.
  • Pack size (pieces or pairs) and number of cartons.

Which other theatre disposables are usually ordered with them?

Caps and shoe covers are usually ordered together with masks, gowns and gloves.

Frequently asked questions

What is a bouffant cap?

A bouffant cap is a loose, full cap meant to hold all of the hair inside. It is described as the most widely used scrub cap in hospitals.

What are shoe covers made of?

Most disposable shoe covers are made of nonwoven fabric such as polypropylene, or plastic film such as polyethylene. Ask the supplier to state the material on the quote.

Are surgical caps medical devices?

In the US, surgical caps are Class I surgical apparel under 21 CFR 878.4040, exempt from FDA premarket notification. Check the current rule in Pakistan for any item you import.

When are shoe covers or boots required?

The US bloodborne pathogens rule requires caps or hoods and shoe covers or boots where gross contamination can reasonably be anticipated, such as autopsies or orthopaedic surgery. Follow your own facility's policy.

Are shoe covers sold in pieces or pairs?

Ask the seller, because pack sizes are not always stated the same way. Confirm whether a pack means pieces or pairs before you compare quotes.
