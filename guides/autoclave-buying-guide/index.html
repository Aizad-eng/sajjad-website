Autoclave buying guide for clinics: class B, N and S explained (EN 13060)

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

For a clinic autoclave, the European standard EN 13060 for small steam sterilisers defines three cycle types. Type N cycles take only unwrapped solid instruments, type S cycles take the loads the maker specifies, and type B cycles take all loads, including wrapped, porous and hollow items such as dental handpieces. Choose by what you will sterilise.

An autoclave is the most expensive item in most small clinics' sterilisation setup, and the wrong cycle type can leave you unable to sterilise the instruments you actually use. This guide explains the class B, N and S cycle types in the European standard EN 13060, what to look for on a specification sheet, and what affects the price. For the wider process, read our sterilisation basics guide.

What is EN 13060?

EN 13060 is the European standard for small steam sterilisers, the tabletop autoclaves used in clinics and dental practices. It sets requirements for these sterilisers and the test loads used to check their performance. A brochure from the Czech maker BMT Medical Technology explains that it applies to sterilisers with a chamber of no more than 60 litres that cannot hold a full sterilisation unit (300 x 300 x 600 mm).

W&H notes that big steam sterilisers fall under a different standard, EN 285. Ask the supplier which edition of EN 13060 the model was tested against, and check the current edition at the time of writing (November 2026).

One detail is often misunderstood. The same BMT brochure points out that EN 13060 defines types of sterilisation cycle, not types of steriliser. So a "class B autoclave" really means a steriliser that offers type B cycles. Many units offer more than one cycle type.

What is the difference between class B, N and S?

Type N cycles handle only unwrapped solid items, type S cycles handle whatever the steriliser maker has specified, and type B cycles handle all load types. The table below summarises descriptions from the maker W&H, BMT and an article by Professor Laurence Walsh published by Henry Schein:

Cycle typeHow air is removedLoads it is meant forWrapped packs that stay sterile in storage
N ("naked" solid)Gravity: steam pushes air outUnwrapped solid instruments onlyNo; items are used straight away
S (specified)Active: steam pulsing or pre-vacuumOnly the loads the steriliser maker lists in its instructionsOnly if the maker lists wrapped loads
B (all loads)Pre-vacuum, usually several vacuum pulsesSolid, porous and hollow items, wrapped or double wrappedYes

Walsh describes N cycles as unsuitable for pouches, textiles and hollow items such as dental handpieces, because gravity air removal is too gentle to clear air from them. W&H gives burs and mirrors as examples of solid items, and handpieces as examples of hollow items.

Which class does a GP, dental or minor surgery clinic need?

Choose the cycle type by listing what you will sterilise and whether it must be stored wrapped. A few rules of thumb follow from the definitions:

  • If you pouch or wrap instruments and store them for later, a type N cycle is not designed for that.
  • If you sterilise hollow items, such as dental handpieces or cannulated instruments, you need a type B cycle, or a type S cycle whose instructions name those exact items.
  • If you sterilise textiles or porous packs, you need a cycle validated for porous loads, which normally means type B.

BMT sums it up this way: with S and N cycles, the clinic itself must check that every load matches what the cycle allows, while a type B cycle is meant for all loads. CDC guidance for dental clinics says handpieces should always be heat sterilised between patients, which is one reason many dental practices choose type B units. What should you check on the specification sheet?

Check the cycle types, chamber size, test programmes, water system and record keeping before you compare prices. A useful checklist:

Also ask about the electrical supply the unit needs and its weight and footprint, so you can plan the bench, the socket and any voltage protection.

Why does the vacuum pump matter?

The vacuum pump removes air from the chamber and the load so steam can reach every surface, and sterilisation cannot happen where air remains. The WHO decontamination manual stresses this point. BMT explains that a fractionated vacuum uses at least three evacuations before sterilisation, and that units with only one vacuum stage are not appropriate for hollow items. Walsh adds that multi-pulse air removal works better and faster than a single phase, especially for items with internal channels. This is one reason two autoclaves that look alike can differ a lot in price.

What tests will you need to run on the autoclave?

You will need routine mechanical, chemical and biological monitoring, plus air removal tests on vacuum models. In summary:

  • Every cycle: read and record the time, temperature and pressure.
  • Every pack: use a chemical indicator inside, and outside if the inner one cannot be seen.
  • Daily, for vacuum (type B) units: an air removal test, using a Bowie-Dick test for porous loads or a helix test for hollow loads, as Walsh describes. CDC runs the Bowie-Dick test at 134 °C for 3.5 minutes before the first load.
  • At least weekly: a biological spore test, as CDC recommends for dental sterilisers.
  • After installation, repair or relocation: repeat testing before use.

Budget for these consumables when you buy the autoclave, not after.

Are pressure-cooker style sterilisers good enough?

Pot-style tabletop sterilisers can sterilise clean, unwrapped instruments, but the items are not sterile once they leave the chamber. WHO describes these units as working like a pressure cooker, with a cycle of about 16 minutes plus about 10 minutes for the pressure to drop. WHO discourages their widespread use outside primary health and dental clinics, and notes that drying is often optional, so instruments may come out wet. If you want to store sterile packs, choose a unit whose cycles are designed for wrapped loads.

What paperwork and after-sales support should come with an autoclave?

Ask for the instructions for use, the list of applied standards, a service manual or service contact, spare parts availability and clear warranty terms. Ask which parts, such as door seals and filters, need regular replacement, and how often the maker recommends servicing. Our guide to medical equipment warranty in Pakistan covers what to ask. For the regulatory side, read our guide to the Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan (DRAP) medical devices rules and check the current rule with the importer.

What changes the price of an autoclave in Pakistan?

The price depends mainly on cycle type, chamber size, vacuum system, drying performance, record-keeping features and brand. BMT notes that units which look alike can differ in price because one has a cheaper, less powerful vacuum pump. Accessories such as a printer, extra trays, a water distiller or a label printer add to the total. For imported units, the rupee exchange rate, shipping and duties on the day of import also change the final figure, so any price is only valid for a short time.

Frequently asked questions

What is a class B autoclave?

It is a small steam steriliser that offers type B cycles under EN 13060. These cycles use pre-vacuum air removal and are meant for all load types, including wrapped, porous and hollow items.

Can a class N autoclave sterilise instrument pouches?

No. Type N cycles are meant only for unwrapped solid instruments, which cannot be kept sterile after the cycle.

Is a class S autoclave as good as class B?

A type S cycle is only suitable for the loads its maker lists in the instructions. It can be the right choice if those loads match your instruments exactly.

Does a dental clinic need a class B autoclave?

Many dental practices choose type B because handpieces are hollow and must be heat sterilised between patients. A type S unit can also work if its instructions cover your handpieces.

What water should be used in a tabletop autoclave?

WHO describes tabletop sterilisers as using distilled or deionised water added by the user. Always follow the steriliser maker's water specification.
