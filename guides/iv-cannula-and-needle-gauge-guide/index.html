IV cannula and needle gauge guide: sizes, colour codes and how to order

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

Gauge runs backwards: the smaller the number, the wider the cannula or needle. Common IV cannula colours are 14G orange, 16G grey, 18G green, 20G pink, 22G blue and 24G yellow. Common hypodermic needle colours include 21G green, 23G blue, 25G orange and 26G brown. Colours can vary by brand, so always read the printed size.

Cannulas and needles are ordered by gauge, and the hub colour helps staff pick the right one quickly. This guide sets out the common gauge sizes and colours for peripheral IV cannulas and hypodermic needles, what reputable sources list as typical uses, and what to write on an order. It is a buying reference. The choice of device for a patient is a clinical decision for your own staff and policies.

What does gauge mean on a cannula or needle?

Gauge is a size number based on the outside diameter. NHS guidance from Salisbury NHS Foundation Trust explains that cannula size is usually expressed in standard wire gauge and that the smaller the gauge number, the larger the diameter. So a 14G cannula is much wider than a 24G cannula.

Needles and cannulas are often labelled with both the gauge and the metric size. For example, a 21G needle is listed by MSF as 0.8 mm outer diameter, and a BD 22G needle is listed as 0.70 mm. When comparing brands, the metric diameter is the more exact figure.

Which standards set the colour codes?

  • ISO 10555-5 covers sterile, single-use over-needle peripheral intravascular catheters, which is the formal name for IV cannulas. It includes a colour code linked to the catheter's outside diameter.
  • ISO 6009:2016 sets the colour coding used to identify single-use hypodermic needles.
  • ISO 7864:2016 covers sterile single-use hypodermic needles, and ISO 9626:2016 covers the stainless steel needle tubing. MSF's catalogue cites both for its needles, together with ISO 6009:2016 and ISO 80369-7:2021 for small-bore connectors.

Even with these standards, colours are not always identical across brands. An anaesthesia training article from a Royal Devon and Exeter NHS clinician notes that different manufacturers use different colour schemes, so staff should know the sizes and flow rates of the products used in their own hospital.

The same article also lists a 17G white cannula with a maximum flow of 125 ml/min, which is less common. The NHS guidance adds a general principle: use the smallest and shortest cannula suitable for the procedure, because complications rise as the cannula gets larger relative to the vein.

How do flow rate and length affect the choice?

Flow through a cannula depends mostly on its internal diameter. The Update in Anaesthesia article explains that flow is proportional to the fourth power of the radius, a simplified form of Poiseuille's equation, and varies inversely with length. In practice, a slightly wider cannula carries much more flow, and a longer cannula of the same gauge carries less.

Because flow also depends on the design, the NHS guidance notes that flow rates vary between manufacturers and are usually printed on the cannula packaging. When comparing quotes, compare the printed flow rate and length, not only the gauge.

The same gauge is sold in several lengths. A 22G needle, for example, is listed at 25 mm by BD and at 30 mm in another common reference, so always state the length you need.

Why do colours sometimes differ between brands?

Hub colours are a quick visual aid, not the final check. BD's own US product pages, for example, list one 23G needle with a turquoise hub and a 30G needle with a tan hub. Cannula colours can also differ, such as 14G cannulas shown as orange or brown.

Shades also differ within one colour name. BD's turquoise 23G hub, next to the blue 23G hub in MSF's catalogue, is one example. Staff who are used to one brand can pick up the wrong size from a new brand if they look only at the colour.

For buying, this has two practical effects. First, when switching brands, check the printed gauge and length on the new packs before they go into stock. Second, if your staff rely on colour, mention it on your order so the supplier can flag any brand that uses a different shade.

What should you specify when ordering IV cannulas?

  1. Gauge and quantity per gauge, for example 200 x 22G, 200 x 20G, 100 x 18G.
  2. Length in mm, since flow and fit depend on it.
  3. Ported or non-ported. Some cannulas have an injection port on top. The Salisbury NHS guidance notes concerns about port caps and contamination and does not recommend giving routine medication through these ports.
  4. Safety or standard design, such as a needle-tip protection feature.
  5. Catheter material and flow rate, as printed by the maker.
  6. Sterile single packs and box quantity.

What should you specify when ordering needles?

  1. Gauge and length, for example 23G x 25 mm.
  2. Luer fitting to match your syringes.
  3. Standard or safety needle. ISO 23908 covers sharps injury protection features, and in 2015 the WHO called for a switch to safety-engineered syringes whenever appropriate.
  4. Sterile, single use, with the standards cited on the pack (for example ISO 7864 and ISO 6009).
  5. Quantity per box and expiry date range.

How many of each size should a clinic stock?

There is no fixed rule, and stock levels depend on your own use. A simple method is to count what each department used over the last three months, by gauge and length, and order that amount plus a margin for delivery time. Because items are imported on order, plan for lead time rather than running stock down to zero. Keep the large-bore cannulas that are used less often on a separate, smaller reorder line so they do not expire unused, and check expiry dates when stock arrives.

Frequently asked questions

Which is bigger, an 18G or a 22G cannula?

An 18G cannula is bigger. Gauge numbers run backwards, so the smaller the number, the wider the cannula.

What colour is a 20G cannula?

In the common scheme listed by NHS guidance, a 20G cannula is pink. Always check the printed gauge, as colours can vary between manufacturers.

What colour is a 23G needle?

A 23G hypodermic needle is commonly blue, as in MSF's catalogue. Some brands use a different shade, for example BD lists one 23G needle with a turquoise hub.

Which standard sets needle colour codes?

ISO 6009:2016 sets the colour coding for single-use hypodermic needles. IV cannulas are covered by ISO 10555-5, which includes its own colour code.

Why does flow rate differ between cannulas of the same gauge?

Flow depends on the internal diameter and the length of the cannula, and designs differ by maker. NHS guidance notes that the flow rate is usually printed on the packaging.
