How to check if a BP apparatus is accurate

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

To check if a BP apparatus is accurate, first confirm the exact model has passed clinical validation, for example on the STRIDE BP list. Then compare it with a known accurate device at a clinic, using several readings. For a manual aneroid gauge, the needle must rest inside the zero mark, but that alone does not prove accuracy.

A blood pressure reading is only useful if the device behind it is accurate. Some BP apparatus give readings that are several points away from the true value, and a gauge can look fine at rest while reading wrongly under pressure. This guide explains what "accurate" means for a BP apparatus, how to check a digital monitor and a manual aneroid gauge, and how often to check. It covers the equipment only. For questions about your own blood pressure, follow your doctor's advice.

What does "accurate" mean for a BP apparatus?

An accurate BP apparatus is one that has passed a clinical validation study against a reference method. The current international protocol is ISO 81060-2:2018, which a 2022 review in the Journal of Human Hypertension calls the "universal standard" for new validation studies. In a validation study, the device's readings are compared with readings taken by trained observers using the listening (auscultation) method.

One of the pass criteria in ISO 81060-2 is that the average difference between the device and the reference must be within plus or minus 5 mmHg, with a standard deviation of no more than 8 mmHg, as described in a 2023 validation paper in Blood Pressure Monitoring. The same 2022 review states that most automated cuff devices sold to consumers worldwide have not been clinically validated. A monitor can therefore carry a medical label and still never have been through this test. Our guide to BP monitor validation explains the protocols in more detail.

How do you check if a digital BP monitor model is validated?

Look up the exact model number on an independent validated device list. Four lists are commonly used, and the 2022 review describes how each one works.

STRIDE BP classes upper-arm devices validated in the last 10 years as "Preferred". At the time of writing (November 2026), its website says it has approved 551 electronic monitors and recommends 294 as preferred, out of more than 5,000 on the market. Because Medaval lists non-validated devices too, read the status shown next to the model rather than assuming it passed.

Match the model number exactly, because the validation evidence applies to the listed model, not to every monitor from the same brand.

How do you compare a home monitor with a clinic device?

Take your monitor to a clinic and compare its readings with a known accurate instrument. Harvard Health Publishing recommends bringing a home monitor to the doctor's office to test it against a known, accurate instrument, repeating the test every year, and taking several readings and averaging them rather than relying on one.

The same article describes a kidney clinic in Ottawa that checked home monitors brought in by 210 patients. In 30% of cases the systolic reading was 5 mmHg or more away from the office reference, and some machines were 15 or 20 mmHg off. A practical comparison looks like this:

  1. Bring the monitor, its cuff and the manual.
  2. Ask the clinic staff to take readings with their reference device and with yours, on the same arm, with you seated in the same position.
  3. Take several readings with each device and compare the averages, not single numbers.
  4. Write down the differences for systolic and diastolic readings and keep the record.
  5. If the differences are large, ask the clinic whether the monitor is suitable to keep using.

For correct measuring technique at home, see our guide on how to check blood pressure at home.

How do you check a manual aneroid BP apparatus?

Start with the zero check, then compare it with a reference gauge. ADC (American Diagnostic Corporation), an aneroid gauge maker, explains the zero check: fully deflate the gauge, preferably disconnected from the bulb and cuff, and look at where the needle rests against the small oval or rectangle printed at zero.

  • Needle outside the zero mark: ADC says the gauge is definitely out of calibration and should not be used until it has been serviced.
  • Needle inside the zero mark: this does not prove accuracy. ADC explains that worn springs or bellows can cause errors that only show at higher pressures, so the gauge should still be checked against a calibrated reference standard.

A 2016 study in the Cardiopulmonary Physical Therapy Journal checked 289 aneroid gauges against a mercury reference and found that 22.5% failed. Of the gauges whose needle sat inside the zero mark, 16.2% still failed. A reference check compares the gauge with a calibrated reference manometer across its range, and is normally a job for a biomedical engineer or a service workshop.

How do the checks differ for digital and manual devices?

Digital monitors are checked mainly by model validation and clinic comparison, while aneroid gauges also need a zero check and periodic reference testing.

How often should a BP apparatus be checked?

Check a home digital monitor against a clinic device about once a year, and an aneroid gauge at least every two years. Harvard Health recommends repeating the clinic comparison every year. ADC recommends checking aneroid gauges at least once every two years, and more often if the gauge is used heavily or has been dropped.

Clinics with several devices should keep a simple log of each device, the date of each check and the result, so nothing is missed.

What makes a BP apparatus read wrongly?

The common causes are an unvalidated design, wear, physical shocks and incorrect use. ADC names worn springs and bellows and dropped gauges as causes of error in aneroid devices. For digital monitors, the 2022 review's point that most consumer devices are unvalidated means the design itself may never have been proven accurate. Always use a cuff from the size range stated in the manual and follow its measuring instructions, since a device is validated for use as directed.

Can you calibrate a digital BP monitor yourself?

No, none of the sources we used describe a calibration step that home users can carry out. If a monitor fails a clinic comparison, contact the manufacturer or its authorised service, or replace it with a validated model. Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my BP machine is accurate?

Check that the exact model is on an independent validated device list such as STRIDE BP, then compare it with a clinic device using several readings on the same arm.

How much difference between two BP machines is acceptable?

Validation under ISO 81060-2 requires an average difference within 5 mmHg with a standard deviation of no more than 8 mmHg. For your own device, ask the clinic whether the difference they find is acceptable.

If the needle of my aneroid gauge rests at zero, is it accurate?

Not necessarily. ADC says a needle inside the zero mark does not confirm calibration, and a 2016 study found 16.2% of such gauges still failed a reference check.

How often should a BP monitor be checked?

Harvard Health suggests comparing a home monitor with a clinic device every year. ADC suggests checking aneroid gauges at least every two years, or sooner after heavy use or a drop.
