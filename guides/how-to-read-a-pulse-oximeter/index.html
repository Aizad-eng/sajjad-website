How to read a pulse oximeter: SpO2, pulse rate and errors

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

A pulse oximeter shows two main numbers: SpO2, the estimated percentage of oxygen in your blood, and PR, your pulse rate in beats per minute. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) says most healthy people read 95% to 100%. Read it after resting, with a warm, still hand and no nail polish, once the numbers stop changing.

A fingertip pulse oximeter is one of the easiest medical devices to use at home, but it is also easy to misread. The numbers can jump around, cold fingers or nail polish can throw them off, and the layout of the screen differs from one model to the next. This guide explains what each number means, how to get a steady reading and what can cause an error, based on guidance from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), NHS England, NHS trusts and Kaiser Permanente. It is about using the device, not about diagnosis. Ask your doctor which readings matter for you.

What do the numbers on a pulse oximeter mean?

A pulse oximeter shows two main numbers: SpO2, the estimated percentage of oxygen in your blood, and PR, your pulse rate in beats per minute.

The FDA explains that the device clips on a fingertip and uses light to estimate oxygen without taking blood. Some models also show a number for signal strength. Do not rely on where a number sits on the screen. On the oximeter described by Cambridge University Hospitals the top number is heart rate and the bottom is SpO2, while the NHS England leaflet shows SpO2 above PR. Always read the label next to each number.

What is a normal SpO2 reading?

The FDA says most healthy people have an SpO2 between 95% and 100%.

The FDA adds that readings can be lower in people with lung or heart conditions and are generally a little lower at high altitude. Kaiser Permanente notes that people with a condition such as COPD may normally run below 95%, and advises asking your doctor which number should prompt a call. Your doctor may give you a personal target. Follow that rather than a general range, and remember that only a health professional can diagnose low oxygen.

How do you take an accurate reading step by step?

Rest first, warm your hand, clip the oximeter on a clean finger without nail polish, keep still and wait until the numbers stop changing.

  1. Wash your hands, as the NHS England leaflet advises. Warm water also warms the fingers.
  2. Remove nail polish or false nails from the finger you will use.
  3. Sit and rest for about 5 minutes. NHS England and Walsall Healthcare NHS Trust both ask for 5 minutes of rest.
  4. Switch the device on and clip it on the index or middle finger, nail facing up.
  5. Keep the hand still and relaxed. Do not squeeze the clip or raise the arm.
  6. Leave it on for at least a minute, or longer if the numbers keep changing, as the Walsall leaflet advises.
  7. Read the numbers once they have stopped changing, and write them down with the date and time.

Sources differ on hand height. The FDA and Kaiser Permanente say to keep the hand below heart level, while the NHS leaflets say to rest the hand on the chest at heart level. Follow your device manual or your doctor, and use the same position every time so readings can be compared.

How do you know the reading is reliable?

A reading is more reliable when the pulse signal is steady and the numbers have stopped moving for several seconds.

The Walsall leaflet asks you to check that the display shows a regular waveform and to record the result once it has not changed for at least 5 seconds. Kaiser Permanente says to wait for one steady number. If the waveform is flat or jumpy, or the numbers keep changing, take the clip off, warm the hand, rest and try again. If you never get a steady reading, check the troubleshooting section of your manual.

What can cause a wrong or error reading?

Movement, cold fingers, nail polish, poor circulation and skin pigmentation are the main causes of inaccurate readings named by health bodies.

Does skin colour affect pulse oximeter readings?

Yes, skin pigmentation can affect accuracy. The FDA says research suggests accuracy differs between lighter and darker skin tones, and Kaiser Permanente warns that darker skin can cause a device to miss low oxygen levels.

In January 2025 the FDA issued draft guidance with recommendations to improve the accuracy of medical-purpose pulse oximeters across all skin pigmentations. At the time of writing (November 2026) check the FDA's site for the current status. For now, the practical advice is the same for everyone: look at how the person feels and looks, not only at the number.

Are all fingertip oximeters medical devices?

No. The FDA separates medical-purpose pulse oximeters from those sold for general wellness, sports or aviation use.

Medical-purpose devices are used mainly in hospitals and clinics, and a small number are cleared by the FDA for over-the-counter sale. Wellness, sports and aviation models are not reviewed by the FDA before sale and are not meant for medical decisions. Kaiser Permanente adds that some home oximeters are sold without research on how well they work. When buying, check the box and manual for the intended use and the stated accuracy. When should you trust symptoms over the number?

Always consider symptoms alongside the reading, because some people with low oxygen have no symptoms and an oximeter can be wrong.

The FDA lists signs of low oxygen that matter even if the screen looks normal:

  • Bluish face, lips or nails
  • Shortness of breath, difficulty breathing or a worsening cough
  • Restlessness or discomfort
  • Chest pain or tightness
  • A fast or racing pulse

The FDA advises contacting a health care provider if you are worried about a reading or if symptoms are serious or getting worse. Kaiser Permanente adds that a downward trend over several readings is a reason to call your doctor. In an emergency, seek medical help straight away.

How should you keep a pulse oximeter log?

Record each reading with the date, time and how you feel, and take readings at the same times each day.

The FDA asks you to record the date and time with each reading, and Kaiser Permanente suggests a log to spot trends. The NHS England leaflet asks patients to note readings at the same time each day and to also note how easy or hard breathing feels. Ask your doctor how often to check, for example at rest, during activity or when symptoms change.

Frequently asked questions

What does PR mean on a pulse oximeter?

PR is pulse rate, the number of heartbeats per minute (bpm). The other main number, SpO2, is the estimated oxygen level in the blood as a percentage.

What is a normal oxygen level on a pulse oximeter?

The FDA says most healthy people read between 95% and 100%. People with lung or heart conditions may run lower, so ask your doctor for your own target.

Which finger is best for a pulse oximeter?

NHS leaflets suggest the index finger or the middle finger. Use a finger with no nail polish or false nail, and keep the nail facing up.

Why does my pulse oximeter reading keep changing?

Movement, cold fingers and a weak pulse signal make the numbers jump. Rest, warm your hand, keep still and wait at least a minute until the numbers settle.

Does nail polish affect pulse oximeter readings?

Yes. The FDA lists fingernail polish among the factors that can affect accuracy, so remove it from the finger you use.
