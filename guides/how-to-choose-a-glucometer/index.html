How to choose a glucometer: accuracy, strips and features

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

To choose a glucometer, first check that it meets the ISO 15197:2013 accuracy standard, then make sure its test strips are easy to buy in Pakistan at a cost you can keep paying. After that, match the features to the user: screen, memory, display unit (mg/dL or mmol/L) and app support. Ask your doctor which meter suits you.

The meter is a one-time purchase, but the test strips are bought again and again, so the right glucometer is the one that is accurate, easy to use and backed by strips you can keep getting. This guide explains the accuracy standard, why strips matter more than the meter, which features are worth having and what to ask before you order. It is a buying guide, not medical advice. Your doctor can tell you how often you need to test.

What matters most when choosing a glucometer?

Accuracy, strip supply and ease of use matter most. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lists these points to consider when picking a meter:

  • accuracy and the amount of blood needed
  • ease of use and how fast results appear
  • size of the meter and how many results it stores
  • medicines that may interfere with the reading
  • data transfer to a computer, phone or cloud service
  • the cost of the meter and the strips
  • technical support from the manufacturer
  • features for people with poor eyesight

The FDA also advises talking to your healthcare provider about the right meter for you.

What does ISO 15197:2013 accuracy mean for a glucometer?

ISO 15197:2013 is the international standard that sets how close a meter's results must be to a laboratory reference. As summarised in a 2019 study in the Journal of Diabetes Science and Technology, the standard requires:

  • at least 95% of results within ±15 mg/dL of the reference when glucose is below 100 mg/dL
  • at least 95% of results within ±15% when glucose is 100 mg/dL or above
  • at least 99% of results inside zones A and B of the consensus error grid, the zones judged clinically acceptable

Some makers publish tighter claims from their own data. For example, Roche states that 95% of Accu-Chek Guide results fall within ±10 mg/dL below 100 mg/dL and within 10% at or above 100 mg/dL, marked as data on file. Treat such figures as the maker's claim and look for ISO 15197:2013 in the meter's leaflet or box. Our guide to ISO 15197 explains the standard in more detail.

Why should test strips decide which glucometer you buy?

Strips decide the long-term cost and whether you can keep testing, because each meter works only with the strips listed in its manual. A low-priced meter is a poor choice if its strips are hard to find or expensive in your city. Before you buy, check:

  • that strips for that exact model are regularly available in Pakistan
  • the strip pack sizes and the expiry dates on current stock
  • whether a use-within period applies after the vial is opened
  • that control solution for the meter can be bought too

Should a glucometer show mg/dL or mmol/L?

Choose the unit your doctor and your laboratory reports use, and check it on the meter screen before you rely on it. Meters sold in the United States report results in mg/dL, according to the FDA, but meters can also be set to mmol/L. Roche has published a notice that some Accu-Chek Guide meters were set to show mmol/L instead of mg/dL, which shows why it is worth checking the unit on a new meter. Reading the wrong unit can lead to a serious misunderstanding of the number.

Do health conditions or medicines affect which glucometer to buy?

They can, so read the strip leaflet and ask your doctor. The FDA says severe dehydration, anaemia, extreme hematocrit (the share of red blood cells in the blood) and high uric acid may affect results. It also lists vitamin C, acetaminophen (paracetamol) and xylose as possible interferences, and says to check the meter's instructions for others. Each strip leaflet states its own limits, so compare them if any of these apply to the user.

Who will use the glucometer, and does that change the choice?

Yes. An older user may need a large display, a backlight and few buttons. A user who travels may want a small meter with an app. The FDA mentions features for visually impaired users as one point to weigh. If you are buying for a clinic where many patients will be tested, say so when you ask for a quote: the FDA notes that over-the-counter meters are for single-patient use.

How do you check a new glucometer after buying it?

Run a control solution test and compare the meter with a laboratory result. The FDA says the control result should fall within the range printed on the strip vial, and suggests taking the meter to your healthcare provider so they can watch your technique and compare it with a lab test. The meter also runs an electronic self-check when switched on and shows an error code if something is wrong. Our step-by-step guide on how to use a glucometer covers technique.

What should you ask the seller before ordering a glucometer?

Ask questions that confirm accuracy, strip supply and support:

  • Which standard does the leaflet state, and is it ISO 15197:2013?
  • Which strips does it use, and what is the expiry date on the strips being supplied?
  • Which unit does the display show, mg/dL or mmol/L?
  • Does the pack include a lancing device, lancets and control solution?
  • Where can the manufacturer's support be reached?

Frequently asked questions

Which glucometer is most accurate?

No single meter is best for everyone. Look for ISO 15197:2013 on the leaflet, which requires 95% of results within ±15 mg/dL below 100 mg/dL or ±15% at higher levels, and treat tighter claims as the maker's own data.

Can I use any brand of strips with my glucometer?

No. Use only the strips listed in your meter's manual, so check that those strips are easy to buy before you choose the meter.

Is a Bluetooth glucometer worth it?

It helps if you or a family member want readings on a phone or want to share them with a doctor. If not, a meter with good memory and a written log works too.

What unit should my glucometer show?

Choose the unit your doctor and laboratory use, mg/dL or mmol/L, and check the screen on a new meter, because meters can be set to either unit.

Should I ask my doctor before buying a glucometer?

Yes. The FDA advises talking to your healthcare provider about the right meter, and your doctor will tell you how often to test.
