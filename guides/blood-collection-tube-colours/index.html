Blood collection tube colours and additives: what each cap colour means

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

Blood collection tube colours show which additive is inside. In the BD Vacutainer range, light blue holds sodium citrate, red is a serum tube with clot activator, gold adds a separating gel, green holds lithium heparin, lavender holds EDTA and grey holds sodium fluoride with potassium oxalate. Colours can differ between makers, so read the label.

Every evacuated blood collection tube carries a coloured cap that tells the person drawing blood what is inside it. The colour is a shortcut, not a guarantee. This guide uses BD Vacutainer, one of the most widely used ranges, as the reference, then shows how another maker colours the same additive differently. It covers tubes as products for laboratories and clinics. Which test needs which tube is always decided by your own laboratory.

What do blood collection tube colours mean?

The cap colour identifies the additive in the tube, and the additive decides whether the sample becomes serum or plasma. Some additives stop blood clotting (anticoagulants such as citrate, heparin and EDTA). Others help it clot quickly (clot activators). One type, sodium fluoride, slows the breakdown of glucose in the sample. Because a wrong additive can spoil a result, laboratories train staff to read both the colour and the printed label.

What is the order of draw for blood tubes?

The order of draw is the sequence in which tubes are filled so that additive from one tube does not contaminate the next. The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) sets it out in its standard GP41, Collection of Diagnostic Venous Blood Specimens. CLSI summarises the order as:

  1. Blood culture bottle or tube
  2. Sodium citrate tube (blue)
  3. Serum tubes, with or without clot activator or gel (red, gold)
  4. Heparin tubes, with or without gel (green)
  5. EDTA tubes, with or without gel (lavender, pink)
  6. Sodium fluoride and potassium oxalate tube (grey)

CLSI adds a point that matters for buyers. Plastic serum tubes with clot activator can interfere with coagulation testing, so only a blood culture, a glass plain serum tube or a plastic serum tube without clot activator should be drawn before the citrate tube. BD's UK tube guide follows GP41 (7th edition, 2017).

How many times should each tube be inverted?

Each tube needs gentle mixing straight after collection, and BD gives a set number of inversions for each type. BD says insufficient mixing can give inaccurate results and lead to a repeat draw. One inversion means turning the tube upside down and back again.

Which tests usually go in which tube?

Your laboratory decides the tube for each test, but hospital tube guides show the usual pattern. A BD tube guide prepared for Gloucestershire Hospitals in the UK, with test choices set by that hospital, lists:

  • Light blue citrate: coagulation tests such as INR, APTT and D-dimer. The guide says the tube must be filled to the minimum fill mark and under-filled samples will not be processed.
  • SST II Advance (rust and gold caps in that guide): general chemistry such as liver and thyroid function tests, and viral and bacterial antibody tests.
  • Green lithium heparin: a short list of special tests in that hospital, such as chromosome analysis.
  • Lavender EDTA: full blood count, blood film and HbA1c, among others.
  • EDTA crossmatch tube: blood group and crossmatch samples.
  • Grey fluoride oxalate: glucose, lactate and blood alcohol.

Another laboratory may use a different tube for the same test. Ask yours for its own list before you place a tube order.

Do tube colours mean the same thing for every manufacturer?

No, cap colours are not the same across all makers, and the label is the only reliable guide. The German maker Sarstedt is a clear example. It sells its S-Monovette K3 EDTA tube with a red cap under its EU colour code and with a violet cap under its ISO colour code. In the BD range, red means a serum tube. So a red cap can mean EDTA from one supplier and serum from another.

Colours can vary even inside one brand. The Gloucestershire BD guide lists SST II Advance tubes with both rust and gold caps for different test groups. If your clinic changes tube supplier, update your wall chart and tell staff before the new stock arrives.

What is the difference between serum, gel and plasma tubes?

A serum tube lets the blood clot so the liquid part can be separated, while a plasma tube contains an anticoagulant so the blood never clots. Red and gold BD tubes give serum. The gold SST adds a polymer gel that sits between the cells and the serum after centrifugation, which makes it easier to pour off or test directly. Green, lavender, light blue and grey tubes give plasma or whole blood. Gel tubes need a centrifuge, so a lab buying them should also plan its centrifuge.

What should a lab or clinic check when ordering blood collection tubes?

Write down the exact tube, not just the colour, so a supplier can match it. For each line, specify:

  • Manufacturer and catalogue number, if you have one in use
  • Additive and, where relevant, concentration (for example 3.2% citrate)
  • Draw volume in mL and tube size in mm (for example 13 x 75 mm)
  • Plastic or glass, and paper or clear label
  • Box and case quantity (BD lists most tubes at 100 per box)
  • Expiry date range on the stock offered
  • Compatible needles and holders, since evacuated tubes work as a system (see our needle gauge chart)

Store tubes as the maker's leaflet says and use them before the expiry date printed on the pack. For a new laboratory, our small laboratory equipment list covers the rest of the setup.

Frequently asked questions

What does a lavender top blood tube contain?

In the BD Vacutainer range, a lavender cap means the tube contains EDTA, for example K2EDTA. Other makers may use a different colour for EDTA, so check the label.

What is the light blue blood tube used for?

The light blue BD tube contains buffered sodium citrate and is used for coagulation samples. Hospital guides say it must be filled to the fill mark.

What is the correct order of draw for blood tubes?

CLSI GP41 gives blood culture first, then citrate, serum, heparin, EDTA and finally fluoride oxalate tubes. The order stops additives carrying over from one tube to the next.

Why do some blood tubes have gel in them?

Gel tubes, such as the BD gold SST, contain a polymer gel that forms a barrier between cells and serum or plasma after centrifugation. This makes the sample easier to separate.

Are blood tube colours the same for every brand?

No. Sarstedt, for example, sells a K3 EDTA tube with a red cap under its EU colour code, while red means a serum tube in the BD range. Always read the additive on the label.
