Hospital bed for home: should you buy or rent one?

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

Whether to buy or rent a hospital bed for home depends mainly on how long it will be needed. Renting can suit a short, predictable recovery if the bed and mattress are in good condition. Buying suits long-term care. Either way, check rail gaps and the mattress, and follow your doctor's advice on the bed type.

When a family member comes home from hospital and cannot easily get in and out of an ordinary bed, the first practical question is often whether to buy a hospital bed or rent one. Both can work. The right answer depends on how long the bed is needed, what it has to do and how well the bed and mattress are kept. This guide sets out the questions to ask and the safety checks that apply either way, using guidance from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the Hospital Bed Safety Workgroup.

What is the main difference between buying and renting a hospital bed for home?

The main difference is who owns the bed and who is responsible for its condition. When you buy, the bed is yours for as long as you need it, you choose the model and mattress, and you can resell or pass it on later. When you rent, you pay for a period of use, the bed goes back at the end, and the rental provider usually handles delivery, collection and repairs under whatever terms you agree.

Neither option changes the safety rules. The FDA defines a hospital bed system as the frame plus its side rails, head and foot boards, mattress and any accessories. Every one of those parts matters, whether the bed is new, used or rented.

How long will the bed be needed?

Expected duration is usually the deciding factor, because rent is paid for every month while a purchase is paid once. A short recovery after an operation points towards renting. Long-term care, such as for an elderly or bed-bound family member, points towards buying.

The difficulty is that recovery time is often uncertain. Ask the treating doctor or physiotherapist how long they expect the bed to be needed and follow their advice. If the answer is "a few weeks", renting avoids paying for a bed you will not use later. If the answer is "many months" or "not sure", work out what rent would add up to over a realistic period and compare it with buying.

How do the costs of buying and renting compare?

They compare by total cost over the time the bed is used, not by the monthly figure alone. Prices change with the bed type, brand, mattress, exchange rate and supplier, so ask for written quotes for both options and compare them over the same number of months.

What type of bed does the patient need?

The patient's condition and the carer's needs decide the type, so start with what the doctor or nurse recommends. FDA guidance on bed entrapment covers manual, hydraulic and electric beds alike, so the safety checks below apply to every type. Ask which positions the patient needs, such as raising the backrest or knees, and whether the bed height must change.

The Hospital Bed Safety Workgroup notes, in its advice to facilities, that height-adjustable electric beds may be the best choice for staff and high-risk patients. At home, height adjustment can help a carer who turns, washes or moves the patient several times a day. If you are comparing powered models, our guide to electric hospital bed prices in Pakistan explains the functions. The same type decision applies whether you rent or buy, so do not accept a different type just because it is available.

What safety risks should you check on any home hospital bed?

The most serious risk is entrapment, where a person is caught, trapped or entangled in gaps in or around the rails, mattress or frame. The FDA reports that between 1 January 1985 and 1 January 2013 it received 901 such incidents, including 531 deaths. Most of the patients were frail, elderly or confused.

FDA guidance identifies seven entrapment zones and gives recommended gap limits for the first four, which account for most reports. The limits are based on the size of a small adult head and neck.

ZoneWhere the gap isFDA recommended limit
Zone 1Within the railLess than 120 mm
Zone 2Under the rail, between rail supports or next to a single supportLess than 120 mm
Zone 3Between the rail and the mattressLess than 120 mm
Zone 4Under the rail at the ends of the railLess than 60 mm, and more than a 60 degree angle

The workgroup's answers on entrapment say beds used in home care should be assessed for entrapment risk just like hospital beds, with periodic reassessment. They also say the people who recommend or supply the bed, including a renter, should be aware of the risk. Reassessment is suggested when rails wobble or are damaged, when a mattress becomes softer, and when rails, mattresses or overlays are changed. These dimensions are based on adults, so check with the supplier before a child uses an adult hospital bed.

What should you check before renting a hospital bed?

Check the condition of every part, because a rented bed has been used before. FDA advice on mattress covers explains that worn covers can let blood and body fluids into the mattress core, where they can leak out when the next patient uses it.

  • Look over the mattress cover for cuts, tears, cracks, pinholes, snags or stains.
  • Ask for the cover to be removed so you can see the inside surface and the mattress itself, including the sides and bottom, for wet spots or staining.
  • Refuse a stained or damaged mattress or cover and ask for a replacement.
  • Check that the mattress fits the frame without a large gap at the rails or the head and foot boards.
  • Shake the side rails. They should lock firmly and not wobble.
  • Test every handset button or crank, and the wheel brakes.
  • Ask for the user manual and get the rental terms in writing.

What should you check before buying a hospital bed?

Check that the bed, rails and mattress are designed to be used together. FDA guidance notes that mattresses are replaced more often than frames, so any new mattress should match the frame it will sit on. The workgroup also says that refurbished beds should meet the same dimensional criteria as new ones.

  • Ask for the bed's dimensions, rail type and the mattress size it is designed for.
  • Ask what the maximum patient weight is, as stated by the manufacturer.
  • For electric beds, ask about the motor, handset, power supply and any battery backup.
  • Ask where spare parts such as handsets, rails and castors will come from.
  • Measure the room, door widths and lift or stair access before the bed arrives.

Does the mattress matter as much as the bed?

Yes, because the FDA treats the mattress as part of the bed system and gaps depend on how well it fits. The workgroup's guide points out that air and water mattresses can collapse at the edges under a person's weight, which may push them towards the rail. FDA recommends that a mattress overlay is used on a bed system that already meets the dimensional limits.

If the patient needs a pressure-relief mattress, plan it with the bed rather than adding it later. See our guides to air mattresses for bed sores and the wider home care equipment list for what else is commonly needed after a hospital stay.

Which option suits which situation?

Renting tends to suit a short and predictable need, a family that does not have space to store a bed afterwards, or a case where the right type is still unclear. Buying tends to suit long-term or open-ended care, a patient who needs a specific type or mattress, and families who want control over the condition and cleaning of the mattress. In both cases, the safety checks above apply, and the patient's doctor should guide the choice of bed type.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to rent or buy a hospital bed?

It depends on how long you need it. Add up the rent, deposit and delivery charges over a realistic period and compare that with the purchase price and any resale value.

Is a rented hospital bed safe to use?

It can be, if the mattress cover is intact and clean, the rails lock firmly and the mattress fits the frame. FDA advice says to replace any mattress cover with stains, damage or wear.

Do I need an electric hospital bed at home?

Not always. Manual beds work for many patients, while height-adjustable electric beds can help carers who move or wash the patient often. Ask the patient's doctor or nurse what the patient needs.

What is bed entrapment?

Entrapment is when a person is caught or trapped in gaps in or around the rails, mattress or frame. FDA guidance sets recommended gap limits for the four zones where most reported cases happened.
