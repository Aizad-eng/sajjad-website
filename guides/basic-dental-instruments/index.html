Basic dental instruments list: names, uses and what to order

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

A basic dental instrument list starts with the examination set: a mouth mirror, an explorer (sharp probe), a periodontal probe and cotton tweezers. Clinics then add filling instruments, scalers and curettes, extraction forceps and elevators, and burs for handpieces. Most are reusable stainless steel and must be cleaned and heat sterilised between patients.

Whether you are setting up a new dental surgery, restocking a teaching clinic or replacing worn instruments, it helps to know the standard names and pattern numbers before you ask for a quote. This guide lists the basic hand instruments by group, explains what each group is for, and covers the points that affect sterilisation and ordering. It is a buying guide, not clinical training. Which instruments are in a basic dental examination set?

A basic examination set contains a mouth mirror, an explorer, a periodontal probe and cotton tweezers. These four items are the ones most clinics need in the largest numbers, because one set is used for almost every patient.

InstrumentCommon patterns or sizesWhat it is forWhat to specify
Mouth mirrorNo. 4 (18 mm head), No. 5 (20 mm head); No. 2 for small spacesIndirect vision, reflecting light and holding back cheeks or tongueHead size, thread type, handle
Explorer (sharp probe)No. 23 "shepherd's hook", 3CH "cowhorn", No. 17, 11/12Tactile checking of tooth surfaces and depositsPattern number, single or double ended
Periodontal probeWilliams, Michigan O, PCP12, NabersMeasuring depths around teeth using millimetre markingsPattern and marking style
Dental tweezers (cotton pliers)Several lengths and tip stylesPlacing and removing cotton rolls and small itemsLength and tip style

Two details catch buyers out. First, mouth mirror heads and handles come in two thread systems that do not fit each other, a US style and a European style, so match new heads to your existing handles. Second, probe markings differ by pattern. A Williams probe has lines at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9 and 10 mm, a Michigan O probe has marks at 3, 6 and 8 mm, and a PCP12 probe has bands that alternate every 3 mm. Order the pattern your clinicians are used to.

Which hand instruments are used for fillings?

Filling (restorative) work uses excavators, chisels, condensers and burnishers. The main groups are:

  • Excavators: spoon-shaped instruments for removing soft material from a cavity.
  • Enamel chisels and hatchets: hand cutting instruments for shaping cavity walls and margins.
  • Condensers (pluggers): for pressing filling material into the cavity; amalgam pluggers are one type.
  • Burnishers: ball, beavertail, cone, pear and football shapes, plus the flat plastic, for polishing and contouring fillings.

What scaling and periodontal instruments does a clinic need?

For scaling, clinics stock scalers and curettes. Fine scalers, such as Drury scalers and the MF 4/5, remove lighter deposits. Heavy scalers, such as Jacquette, cushion and hoe scalers, are for heavy tartar and stains. Universal curettes are double ended, with a rounded toe and two level cutting edges, and can be used on all tooth surfaces. Gracey curettes have a semicircular tip with one edge lower than the other, and are used at a different angle to the root.

The CDC classes periodontal scalers as critical items, because they can penetrate soft tissue. That means they should always be heat sterilised after every patient, so you need enough sets to cover the time your sterilisation cycle takes.

Which instruments are used for tooth extraction?

Extraction needs a set of forceps shaped for different teeth, plus elevators. Forceps are sold by pattern, for example upper universal, lower universal, upper molar (left and right), upper canine, root forceps, bayonet and cowhorn. Elevators loosen teeth or roots before or instead of forceps. Common types include:

  • Straight elevators, such as Coupland's and Warwick James.
  • Triangular elevators, such as Cryer's and Winter's.
  • Pick elevators for root tips.
  • Luxators, which are thinner and sharper than conventional elevators.

What about handpieces and burs?

Handpieces drive burs and come as high-speed air turbines and slow-speed handpieces, with straight and contra-angle versions. Bur shanks must match the handpiece: friction grip burs go in high-speed handpieces, latch-type burs in slow-speed contra-angles, and long-shank burs in straight slow-speed handpieces. Bur heads are made of tungsten carbide, diamond-coated steel or plain steel. Round burs run from size 1/4 to 10, and inverted cone burs from 33 1/2 to 90L. When ordering, give the shank type, head shape, size and grit or blade type.

CDC guidance says dental handpieces, low-speed motors and reusable prophy angles should be heat sterilised between patients, not just wiped. Check that any handpiece you buy is labelled as autoclavable and comes with reprocessing instructions.

Which root canal (endodontic) instruments are on a basic list?

A basic endodontic list covers hand files, spreaders and pluggers, plus an endodontic explorer. Files come in several designs, including K-files, Hedstrom (H) files, manual and rotary tapered files, and nickel-titanium (NiTi) flex files. Finger spreaders and finger pluggers are small hand-held instruments that sit alongside the files. Larger items, such as an apex locator or a dental microscope, are equipment rather than hand instruments. Files are ordered by type, length and size range, so ask the supplier for the exact range in each box.

How are dental instruments classified for sterilisation?

Dental instruments are classed as critical, semicritical or noncritical by what they touch. The WHO decontamination manual and the CDC dental summary give these examples:

CategoryWhat it touchesDental examplesProcessing
CriticalSoft tissue, bone or bloodExtraction forceps, elevators, flap retractors, surgical burs, periodontal scalersSterilise; use single-use items once
SemicriticalMucous membranesMouth mirrors, restorative instruments, tweezers, probes, amalgam condensers, metal impression traysHeat sterilise between patients if heat tolerant
NoncriticalIntact skinWillis gauge, face bow, protective eyewear, bib chainsClean, and disinfect if soiled

WHO states that steam under pressure is the preferred method for all dental instruments, and that ultraviolet light and boiling water do not sterilise instruments. Read our sterilisation basics guide and autoclave buying guide for the equipment side.

How many sets of each instrument should a clinic order?

Order enough sets to cover every patient seen during one full cleaning and sterilisation turnaround, plus spares. Because critical and most semicritical instruments are sterilised after every patient, each chair needs sets in use, sets being cleaned, sets in the autoclave and sets cooling or in storage. Count your patients per session and your full cycle time, including drying and cooling, then add spares for loss and repair. WHO mentions instrument cassettes as one way to keep processed sets together in storage.

What should you check when buying dental hand instruments?

Check the material, the pattern, the fit with your existing items and the reprocessing instructions. ISO 7153-1:2016 covers metals for standard surgical instruments, including those used in dentistry, and ISO 13402 sets tests for how surgical and dental hand instruments resist autoclaving, corrosion and heat. Our guide to stainless steel grades explains why cutting instruments use hardened martensitic steel.

  • Ask for each item by name and pattern number, for example "explorer No. 23" or "Williams periodontal probe".
  • Match mirror thread type and handle style to what you already use.
  • Ask whether cutting instruments can be resharpened.
  • Ask for the maker's cleaning and sterilisation instructions. CDC notes that if a maker gives no reprocessing instructions, the device may not be suitable for use on more than one patient.

Frequently asked questions

What are the four basic dental examination instruments?

A mouth mirror, an explorer (sharp probe), a periodontal probe and cotton tweezers. Most clinics order these in the largest numbers because they are used for nearly every patient.

What size dental mirror is most common?

The most common mouth mirror heads are No. 4, about 18 mm across, and No. 5, about 20 mm. A No. 2 is used where a smaller mirror is needed.

Do mouth mirror heads fit any handle?

No. There are two thread systems, a US style and a European style, and they do not fit each other. Match new heads to your handles.

What is the difference between a universal and a Gracey curette?

A universal curette has two level cutting edges and can be used on all tooth surfaces. A Gracey curette has one edge set lower than the other and is used at a different angle.

Can dental instruments be sterilised by boiling?

No. The WHO manual states that boiling water and ultraviolet light do not sterilise instruments, and names steam under pressure as the preferred method for dental instruments.
