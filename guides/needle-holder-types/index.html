Needle holder types and how to choose one for your clinic

Updated November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

A needle holder, also called a needle driver, is a hinged instrument that grips a curved suture needle. Mayo-Hegar is the standard general pattern, Kilner and Halsey are common in minor sets, Olsen-Hegar and Gillies add built-in scissors, and Castroviejo suits fine work. Choose by suture size, length, ratchet, jaw type and tungsten carbide inserts.

Needle holders come in many patterns and lengths, and catalogue names alone do not tell you which one fits your sutures. This guide explains how needle holders are built, compares the common patterns with their lengths and suture size ranges, and lists what to write when you order. It covers equipment only. Suturing technique belongs to clinical training.

What is a needle holder?

A needle holder, also called a needle driver, is a surgical instrument used to hold and drive a suture needle while closing a wound or joining tissue. It looks like a haemostat but is built to grip a needle firmly.

A needle holder has three main parts:

  • Jaws: short and textured to grip the needle, often reinforced with tungsten carbide inserts.
  • Joint: the hinge that connects jaws and handles.
  • Handles: usually ending in finger rings, with a ratchet on most patterns.

Short jaws and long handles give a lever effect for a firmer grip on the needle. WHO lists needle holders as one of four broad instrument groups, alongside forceps, scissors and retractors, so every surgical or minor set needs its own.

Most needle holders are held like scissors, using finger rings. The University of Bristol clinical skills sheet describes the thumb in one ring, the ring finger (or middle finger for large hands) in the other and the index finger along the handle near the hinge. WHO adds that the driver should be held so the fingers can come free of the rings, letting the wrist turn the needle. Ring size and handle length therefore affect comfort, so consider ordering one sample before buying in bulk.

What are the main types of needle holders?

The main types are Mayo-Hegar, Kilner, Halsey, Olsen-Hegar, Crile-Wood, Derf, Webster, Castroviejo and Gillies. WPI also lists a 14 cm smooth jaw needle holder for 5-0 to 7-0 sutures and a 16 cm titanium needle driver for 1-0 to 4-0 sutures. The Mathieu needle holder is another specialty pattern.

What is the difference between Mayo-Hegar and Olsen-Hegar needle holders?

A Mayo-Hegar is a plain needle holder, while an Olsen-Hegar combines a needle holder with scissor blades so the user can cut the suture with the same instrument.

WPI describes the Mayo-Hegar as having a long, slender body, finger rings, serrated cross-hatched jaws and a ratchet lock, made of surgical grade stainless steel suitable for autoclaving. Its Olsen-Hegar range runs from 12 to 16.5 cm, in plain and tungsten carbide versions. A combined instrument means the user does not need to pick up separate scissors to cut the suture.

What are Gillies needle holders?

Gillies needle holders have no ratchet, so the user holds the needle with steady pressure instead of locking it. The University of Bristol clinical skills sheet notes that there is a knack to holding them without letting the needle slip.

Gillies needle holders are also handed. The same sheet says left-handed users will struggle with right-handed Gillies and the reverse, while Mayo needle holders can be used by both. Surgical Holdings sells Gillies and Foster Gillies patterns as combined scissor and needle holders. If anyone on your team is left-handed, say so in your order.

What is a Castroviejo needle holder?

A Castroviejo needle holder is a fine needle holder held with a tweezer-like grip, which allows small, precise movements. It is named after the eye surgeon Ramon Castroviejo and is used in eye surgery, microsurgery and dental surgery. WPI lists 14 cm Castroviejo needle holders with smooth jaws, carbide jaws and in titanium, and 9 cm micro versions for the finest sutures.

How do you match a needle holder to suture size?

Match the needle holder to the suture and needle you use most, because jaws that are too large or too fine will not hold the needle well. In the WPI chart, the 15 cm Mayo-Hegar is matched to 1-0 to 4-0 sutures, patterns such as Halsey and Olsen-Hegar to 3-0 to 6-0, and micro Castroviejo and Webster patterns to 8-0 to 10-0.

WHO, in its manual Surgical Care at the District Hospital, describes holding the needle with the tip of the driver between half and two thirds of the way along the needle. A needle holder whose jaws suit the needle makes that grip easier. Our sutures guide explains suture types if you are building a full suturing setup.

What are tungsten carbide needle holders?

Tungsten carbide needle holders have hard carbide inserts in the jaws for a firmer grip and longer life, and they are often marked with gold-plated handles. Eickemeyer describes its 15 cm Mayo-Hegar with tungsten carbide inserts as having a pyramid-shaped profile that holds the needle especially firmly, inserts that can be replaced when they wear, and gold-plated handles.

Plain stainless steel jaws are the other option. If a needle holder will be used many times a day, ask whether the carbide inserts can be replaced, as they can on the Eickemeyer model.

Should a needle holder have a ratchet?

Most needle holders have a ratchet that locks the handles and clamps the needle, so the user does not have to keep squeezing while passing it through tissue. The University of Bristol sheet notes that the ratchet on Mayo needle holders helps stop the needle slipping or twisting. Gillies needle holders are the main ratchet-free option.

What should you check before ordering a needle holder?

Check the pattern, length, jaw type and suture range, then test the grip when the instrument arrives.

  • Length: WHO advises choosing the shortest instrument that will comfortably reach the operative site.
  • Grip: WHO warns that needle drivers that do not grip the needle securely are frustrating and dangerous. Clamp a needle on arrival and try to turn it.
  • Do not substitute: artery forceps should not be used as needle holders, so order enough needle holders for every set.
  • Standard: ISO 7151 covers non-cutting articulated (jointed) surgical instruments, which describes a needle holder. Ask the supplier which standard applies. The edition listed at the time of writing (November 2026) is ISO 7151:2024.
  • Write it out in full: for example "Mayo-Hegar, 15 cm, tungsten carbide jaws, ratchet, quantity 10".

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common type of needle holder?

The Mayo-Hegar is the standard general-purpose needle holder, with serrated jaws and a ratchet lock. Kilner and Halsey patterns are common in minor surgery sets.

What is the difference between a needle holder and a needle driver?

There is no difference. Needle driver and needle forceps are other names for the same instrument, which holds a suture needle during suturing.

What do gold handles on a needle holder mean?

Gold handles often mark tungsten carbide inserts in the jaws. Some makers, such as Eickemeyer, use replaceable carbide inserts that can be changed when they wear.

Can left-handed users use any needle holder?

Ratcheted patterns such as the Mayo needle holder can be used by both hands. Gillies needle holders are handed, so left-handed users need a left-handed pair.

Which needle holder is used for fine sutures?

Fine patterns such as Castroviejo, Webster and micro Castroviejo are matched to fine sutures. One supplier's chart pairs the micro Castroviejo with 8-0 to 10-0 sutures.
