Patient monitor price in Pakistan: parameters and accessories

Updated November 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

Patient monitor price in Pakistan depends mainly on the parameters it measures, from basic ECG, SpO2, NIBP, temperature and respiration to add-ons such as invasive pressure and EtCO2. Screen size, fixed or modular design, SpO2 technology, central station links, accessories, brand and the exchange rate also matter. SurgiSource quotes today's price in rupees through its quote form.

A patient monitor shows a patient's vital signs on one screen and raises alarms when readings move outside set limits. In Pakistan, buyers range from small clinics wanting a basic bedside unit to hospitals equipping an ICU with modular monitors and a central station. This guide explains the main types, the parameters and accessories that drive cost, and what to ask a supplier.

What is a multiparameter patient monitor?

A multiparameter patient monitor is a single device that measures two or more physiological signals from one patient, such as ECG, oxygen saturation and blood pressure. That is how the standard IEC 80601-2-49 defines its scope. According to BSI, it covers monitors used in professional healthcare facilities, emergency medical services and home healthcare, but not single-parameter devices such as a standalone pulse oximeter.

BSI lists the 2019 European edition of this standard as withdrawn in October 2024, so ask suppliers which edition their declaration of conformity names and check the current rule.

Which parameters come as standard and which are optional?

Most bedside monitors come with five basic parameters, and the rest are paid options. Edan's iM20, for example, lists ECG, respiration, non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), SpO2 and temperature as standard. Its options include sidestream EtCO2 (carbon dioxide at the end of each breath) using a Respironics LoFlo module, 12-lead ECG and two channels of invasive blood pressure (IBP).

What is the difference between vital signs, bedside, transport and modular monitors?

The four types differ in how many parameters they carry, how they are powered and where they are used. Examples from Edan and Mindray show the range:

  • Vital signs monitor: Edan's iM3s is a portable vital signs monitor with NIBP and SpO2, and temperature as an option, for adult, paediatric and newborn use. Some versions run on AA batteries.
  • Bedside monitor: Mindray's ePM series comes in compact and modular forms and is aimed at emergency rooms, recovery units, sub-acute units and general wards.
  • Transport monitor: Edan describes the iM20 as weighing under 1.5 kg with the battery fitted, with IP44 water and dust protection and 1.2 metre shock resistance.
  • Modular monitor: Edan's Elite V6 has a 15 inch touch screen and a rack for plug-in modules, and lists BIS among its extra monitoring options for critical care and anaesthesia.

A modular monitor costs more at the start but lets you add modules later. A fixed monitor is simpler and usually cheaper, but you cannot add parameters it was not built with.

Also compare memory and trend review. Edan's iM20 brochure lists built-in storage for 150 hours of trends, 1,200 sets of NIBP readings, 200 alarm events, 200 arrhythmia events and 50 12-lead reports, which helps staff review what happened during a shift or a transfer. Ask how long trends are kept and whether data can be exported or sent to the hospital system before you compare prices.

Does the SpO2 technology in a monitor matter?

Yes, the SpO2 technology affects which sensors fit and how readings behave with movement or poor circulation. Mindray offers its ePM monitors with Mindray, Masimo or Nellcor SpO2, depending on region, and Edan offers its own iMAT algorithm with Nellcor as an option on the iM20. Sensors can be specific to the SpO2 technology, so ask which sensors the monitor takes before you order spares.

The US FDA notes that pulse oximeter readings can be affected by circulation, skin pigmentation, skin thickness, skin temperature, tobacco use, nail polish and movement. Pulse oximeter equipment is covered by ISO 80601-2-61. For more, read our guide to pulse oximeter accuracy.

Which accessories and consumables should you budget for?

Accessories are a large part of the real cost of a monitor, because many are single use or wear out. Plan for:

  • ECG lead cables and disposable electrodes.
  • SpO2 sensors in each patient size you treat.
  • NIBP cuffs in several sizes, including child and newborn if needed.
  • Temperature probes.
  • For EtCO2, sampling lines and water traps. Edan lists G2 water traps as a separate accessory.
  • For IBP, pressure transducers.
  • A recorder or printer kit and thermal paper, if you want printed strips. Edan sells a thermal printer kit separately.
  • A wall mount, rollstand or trolley.

Do you need a central station and network connection?

A central station is worth it when nurses watch several beds from one desk, as in an ICU or high dependency unit. Edan's monitors connect to its MFM-CMS central monitoring station over cable or built-in Wi-Fi, and send data to hospital systems by HL7. Mindray's ePM monitors connect to its CMS and eGateway by wired or wireless network and output HL7 to third-party records systems.

A central station, its software and the network work add cost. A single clinic room seldom needs it.

What changes the patient monitor price in Pakistan?

The price of a patient monitor in Pakistan is set mainly by its parameters and design, then by brand, accessories and import costs. The main factors are:

  • Parameters: basic five versus added IBP, EtCO2, 12-lead ECG or others.
  • Design: vital signs, fixed bedside, transport or modular.
  • Screen: size and touch control.
  • SpO2 technology: the maker's own or a third-party technology such as Masimo or Nellcor.
  • Connectivity: central station, Wi-Fi and HL7 links.
  • Battery and build: run time and protection for transport use.
  • Accessories: sensors, cuffs, cables and mounts in the package.
  • New or refurbished: see refurbished vs new medical equipment.
  • Exchange rate, freight, duty and quantity: monitors are imported, so the rupee rate on the order day matters, and multi-bed orders can change the unit cost.

Because these move often, at the time of writing (November 2026) SurgiSource does not publish fixed prices. We quote the price on the day you ask.

What should you ask before ordering a patient monitor?

  1. Which parameters are standard, and which are paid options?
  2. Can parameters be added later, or is the configuration fixed?
  3. Which SpO2 technology does it use, and which sensors fit?
  4. Which cuff sizes, sensors and cables are in the box?
  5. How long does the battery last, and does it suit transport?
  6. Does it connect to a central station, and at what cost?
  7. Which standards does the declaration of conformity name, such as IEC 80601-2-49 and ISO 80601-2-61?
  8. What warranty applies, and are spare accessories easy to reorder?

Frequently asked questions

What parameters does a basic patient monitor measure?

A basic bedside monitor usually measures ECG, respiration, SpO2, non-invasive blood pressure and temperature. Invasive blood pressure and EtCO2 are usually paid options.

What is the difference between a modular and a fixed patient monitor?

A modular monitor takes plug-in modules, so parameters can be added later. A fixed monitor comes with a set configuration that cannot be expanded, which usually makes it simpler and cheaper.

Can I use any SpO2 sensor with my monitor?

Not always. Sensors can be matched to the monitor's SpO2 technology, such as the maker's own, Masimo or Nellcor, so check compatibility before buying spares.

Do I need a central monitoring station?

A central station helps when staff watch several beds from one desk, as in an ICU. A single clinic room or spot-check use seldom needs one.
